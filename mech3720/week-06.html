Vapour Power Systems (Rankine Cycle)

Most of the world's electricity comes from a single idea: boil a fluid, expand it through a turbine, condense it, and pump it back. This is the Rankine cycle. Because the working fluid changes phase, and because pumping a liquid is cheap, it converts heat to power far more practically than a gas cycle — and every component is just a steady-flow device from Topic 3.

The four components

Number the states 1 (turbine inlet) → 2 (condenser inlet) → 3 (pump inlet) → 4 (boiler inlet). Applying the steady-flow energy equation to each — neglecting KE, PE — gives one enthalpy difference apiece:

  • Turbine (adiabatic): $\dot W_t/\dot m = h_1 - h_2$ — the work out.
  • Condenser (no work): $\dot Q_{out}/\dot m = h_2 - h_3$ — heat rejected.
  • Pump (adiabatic): $\dot W_p/\dot m = h_4 - h_3$ — the work in.
  • Boiler (no work): $\dot Q_{in}/\dot m = h_1 - h_4$ — heat added.

The ideal Rankine cycle

The ideal cycle assumes no frictional pressure drop, no stray heat transfer and reversible components: isentropic turbine and pump, isobaric boiler and condenser. Its thermal efficiency is the net work over the heat added:

$$\eta = \frac{\dot W_t - \dot W_p}{\dot Q_{in}} = \frac{(h_1 - h_2) - (h_4 - h_3)}{h_1 - h_4}$$

Because the pump compresses a liquid — nearly incompressible — its work is well approximated by $h_4 - h_3 \approx v_3(p_4 - p_3)$, which is tiny beside the turbine work. That gives the cycle its low back-work ratio, the key practical advantage over gas cycles.

$$bwr = \frac{\dot W_p}{\dot W_t} = \frac{h_4 - h_3}{h_1 - h_2}$$

Raising the efficiency

The Carnot insight — add heat hot, reject it cold — points to two levers, both limited in practice:

  • Higher boiler pressure raises the average temperature of heat addition and so the efficiency, but places steep demands (and cost) on materials.
  • Lower condenser pressure lowers the rejection temperature. The floor is the saturation pressure at the available cooling-water temperature; it also must stay above ambient to avoid air ingress.

Superheat and reheat

Superheating the steam above saturation before the turbine raises efficiency and keeps the turbine-exit quality high (wet steam erodes blades). Reheat takes partly expanded steam back to the boiler and expands it a second time, extending both benefits. Both are standard on real plants.

Real-cycle imperfections

The largest departure from ideal is the turbine, captured by its isentropic efficiency $\eta_t = (h_1 - h_2)/(h_1 - h_{2s})$; the pump has its own. Further losses come from frictional pressure drops in every component, and the biggest external irreversibility — heat transfer across the large temperature difference between combustion products and the working fluid.

Where this leads Topic 7 turns to cycles whose working fluid stays a gas — where the back-work ratio is high and no phase change helps.
