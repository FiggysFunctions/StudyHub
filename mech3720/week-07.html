Gas Power Systems

In a gas power cycle the working fluid never changes phase — think car engines, diesel generators and jet turbines. The absence of a phase change means the compression device works on a gas, not a liquid, so the back-work ratio is high. To make the combustion tractable we replace it with the air-standard idealisation.

Air-standard analysis

Real combustion is hopelessly complex — reaction kinetics, heat losses, friction, charging and exhaust. The air-standard model cuts through it:

  • a fixed mass of air, modelled as an ideal gas, is the working fluid;
  • combustion is replaced by heat transfer from an external source;
  • intake and exhaust are ignored (the cycle is closed);
  • all processes are internally reversible.

Cold air-standard analysis adds one more simplification — constant specific heats evaluated at ambient temperature — which yields the clean algebraic efficiencies below.

Reciprocating-engine terms

For piston engines: the bore is the cylinder diameter and the stroke the piston travel between top dead centre (TDC) and bottom dead centre (BDC). The compression ratio and mean effective pressure are the two headline numbers:

$$r = \frac{V_{BDC}}{V_{TDC}} \qquad mep = \frac{W_{net}}{V_{max} - V_{min}}$$

The mean effective pressure is a size-independent measure of output — the constant pressure that would give the same net work over one displacement.

The Otto cycle (spark ignition)

The Otto cycle models the petrol engine as four internally reversible processes: isentropic compression (1-2), constant-volume heat addition (2-3, combustion), isentropic expansion (3-4, the power stroke) and constant-volume heat rejection (4-1, exhaust). A closed-system energy balance on each adiabatic stroke gives the work from $\Delta u$ alone, and the cold-air-standard efficiency collapses to a strikingly simple form:

$$\eta_{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,k-1}}$$

Efficiency depends only on the compression ratio and $k = 1.4$. Higher $r$ is always better — but is limited by knock (premature auto-ignition), roughly $r \lesssim 11.5$.

The Diesel and Dual cycles

The Diesel cycle (compression ignition) differs in one process: heat is added at constant pressure (2-3) instead of constant volume, characterised by the cut-off ratio $r_c = V_3/V_2$. Its efficiency carries an extra bracketed factor:

$$\eta_{Diesel} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,k-1}}\left[\frac{r_c^{\,k} - 1}{k\,(r_c - 1)}\right]$$

The bracket is always greater than one, so at equal compression ratio a Diesel is slightly less efficient than an Otto cycle — but compression-ignition engines run at much higher $r$ (no knock limit), so in practice they win on efficiency. The Dual cycle adds heat partly at constant volume and partly at constant pressure, bridging the two.

Gas turbines and jet propulsion

The gas-turbine (Brayton) plant uses steady-flow components — compressor, combustor, turbine — with the same high back-work ratio. In aircraft propulsion the goal shifts from shaft power to thrust: a turbojet compresses and heats air, then expands it through a nozzle to a high exit velocity. The thrust follows from the momentum equation, which at steady state reduces to $F \approx \dot m\,(\mathrm{v}_e - \mathrm{v}_i)$ — so we maximise mass-flow rate and exit velocity. Reheat (an afterburner) adds thrust at the cost of fuel.
