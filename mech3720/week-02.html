Properties of Pure Substances

The energy balance is only useful once we can put numbers to $u$, $h$ and $v$. This topic is about the material model: how to read the state of a pure substance off the property tables, how to handle a wet liquid–vapour mixture through its quality, and when the ideal-gas shortcut applies instead.

Phases and the state principle

Heat a solid and it melts, then boils, then superheats. On a $p$–$v$–$T$ surface these show up as the compressed-liquid, two-phase (wet) and superheated-vapour regions separated by the saturation lines. The key working rule: for a simple compressible substance, two independent intensive properties fix the state. Inside the two-phase dome, though, $p$ and $T$ are not independent (they are locked together at saturation) — so a second property such as quality is needed.

Reading the tables

The saturation tables (A-2 by temperature, A-3 by pressure) list the saturated-liquid (subscript $f$) and saturated-vapour (subscript $g$) values of $v$, $u$, $h$ and $s$ along the saturation line. The superheated tables are indexed by $p$ and $T$ together; the compressed-liquid tables by $p$ and $T$ as well. When a target value falls between table rows, linearly interpolate.

Compressed liquid shortcut If compressed-liquid data are unavailable, approximate the property by its saturated-liquid value at the same temperature: $v \approx v_f(T)$, $u \approx u_f(T)$. Liquids are nearly incompressible, so pressure barely moves them.

Quality and wet mixtures

Inside the dome the mixture is part liquid, part vapour. Its quality is the vapour mass fraction, and every specific property is the quality-weighted average of its $f$ and $g$ endpoints:

$$x = \frac{m_{vap}}{m_{liq}+m_{vap}} \qquad v = v_f + x\,(v_g - v_f), \quad u = u_f + x\,(u_g - u_f), \quad h = h_f + x\,(h_g - h_f)$$

The same lever rule gives entropy, $s = s_f + x(s_g - s_f)$, which we lean on heavily in Topic 5. Rearranged, any measured property tells you the quality — e.g. $x = (v - v_f)/(v_g - v_f)$.

Enthalpy

The combination $h = u + pv$ appears so often it gets its own name and symbol. Enthalpy bundles internal energy with the flow-work group $pv$; it is the natural energy carried by a flowing stream (Topic 3) and it is what changes in a constant-pressure process.

The ideal-gas model

At low density a gas obeys the ideal-gas equation of state, and its internal energy and enthalpy depend on temperature alone:

$$pV = mRT \qquad R = \frac{\bar R}{M}, \quad \bar R = 8.314\ \tfrac{\text{kJ}}{\text{kmol}\cdot\text{K}} \qquad \Delta u = c_v\Delta T, \quad \Delta h = c_p\Delta T$$

The two specific heats are linked by Mayer's relation $c_p - c_v = R$, and their ratio $k = c_p/c_v$ (1.4 for air) drives every isentropic result later. When $c_p$ varies appreciably, use the tabulated $u(T)$ and $h(T)$ from the air table instead of the constant-$c$ forms.

Incompressible substances For a solid or liquid the two specific heats merge into one, $c$, and $\Delta u = c\,\Delta T$ with $\Delta h = c\,\Delta T + v\,\Delta p$ — the pressure term matters for pump work in Topic 6.
Where this leads With property data in hand, Topic 3 applies the energy balance to flowing devices, and Topic 5 uses the same tables for entropy.
