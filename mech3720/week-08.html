Refrigeration & Heat Pump Systems

Run a power cycle backwards and it becomes a device that pumps heat up a temperature gradient, driven by work. A fridge and a heat pump are the same machine — they differ only in which reservoir you care about: the cold one you are cooling, or the warm one you are heating.

The Carnot limit

The Clausius statement (Topic 4) says heat will not flow from cold to hot by itself; a reverse cycle makes it happen at the cost of work input. The best possible performance is again set by the two reservoir temperatures:

$$\beta_{max} = \frac{T_C}{T_H - T_C} \qquad \gamma_{max} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_C}$$

The Carnot refrigeration cycle is the power cycle in reverse — isothermal and adiabatic processes traversed the opposite way. Both coefficients grow as the two temperatures approach one another, which is why moving heat across a small gap is cheap and across a large one is dear.

Coefficient of performance

The figure of merit is the coefficient of performance — benefit over cost. For a refrigerator the benefit is heat removed from the cold space; for a heat pump it is heat delivered to the warm space:

$$\beta = \frac{Q_C}{W_{cycle}} \qquad \gamma = \frac{Q_H}{W_{cycle}} = \beta + 1$$

Both are typically greater than one — the whole point is to move more heat than the work you put in. The heat-pump COP always beats the refrigeration COP of the same cycle by exactly one, since $Q_H = Q_C + W_{cycle}$.

The vapour-compression cycle

The practical workhorse. A refrigeration cycle circulates a refrigerant through four components:

  • Evaporator — the refrigerant boils at low pressure, absorbing $Q_C$ from the cold space.
  • Compressor — raises the pressure (the work input $W_{cycle}$).
  • Condenser — the refrigerant condenses at high pressure, rejecting $Q_H$ to the surroundings.
  • Expansion (throttling) valve — drops the pressure back, essentially isenthalpically (Topic 3).

The same non-idealities as any cycle apply: finite temperature differences in the heat exchangers, compressor isentropic efficiency below one, and frictional pressure losses.

Heat pumps and their sources

A heat pump is structurally identical — only the useful output changes to the condenser heat. Its heat source matters for performance: outside air is most common (but its COP falls as the air gets colder), while the ground, a body of water, a solar collector or waste heat offer a warmer, steadier source and hence a better $\gamma$.

Gas refrigeration

Instead of a phase-changing refrigerant, a gas (reverse-Brayton) refrigeration cycle compresses and expands a gas. Compared with vapour compression it has lower pressures and mass-flow rates but a lower COP; its advantage is reaching much lower temperatures (down to around $-150^{\circ}\text{C}$), being compact, and avoiding any freezing of the working fluid — which is why it appears in aircraft air-conditioning and cryogenics.
