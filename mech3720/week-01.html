Energy, Work & Heat — the First Law for Closed Systems

Thermodynamics is bookkeeping for energy. Before any cycle can be analysed we need the vocabulary — systems, properties, states — and the three ways energy is stored or moved: as internal, kinetic and potential energy; as work; and as heat. The first law ties them together into an energy balance.

Systems, properties and state

A system is whatever we draw a boundary around; everything else is the surroundings. A closed system holds a fixed mass (no matter crosses the boundary), while a control volume lets mass flow through — that is Topic 3. A property (pressure, temperature, specific volume…) depends only on the current state, not the path taken to reach it. For a simple compressible substance, fixing any two independent intensive properties fixes the state, and everything else follows from tables or a model.

Forms of energy

A system's total energy is its microscopic internal energy plus its macroscopic kinetic and potential energies:

$$E = U + KE + PE \qquad \Delta KE = \tfrac{1}{2}m(\mathrm{v}_2^2 - \mathrm{v}_1^2) \qquad \Delta PE = mg(z_2 - z_1)$$

In most closed-system problems the mechanical terms are negligible next to $\Delta U$, so the first law reduces to a statement about internal energy.

Work

Work is energy transfer that could, in principle, have raised a weight. For a simple system the important mode is boundary (expansion/compression) work — the area under the process path on a p–V diagram:

$$W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p\,dV$$

Many real compressions and expansions are modelled as a polytropic process, $pV^{\,n}=\text{const}$, whose work integral has two cases depending on whether $n=1$.

Sign convention This course takes heat into the system as positive and work done by the system as positive — the convention on the formula sheet. (Some textbooks flip the sign of work, so watch out.)

Heat and its three modes

Heat is energy transfer driven by a temperature difference. It moves by three modes, each with a rate law you met in fluid mechanics and will meet again in heat transfer:

$$\underbrace{\tfrac{\dot Q}{A}=-k\tfrac{dT}{dx}}_{\text{conduction}} \qquad \underbrace{\tfrac{\dot Q}{A}=h(T_b-T_f)}_{\text{convection}} \qquad \underbrace{\tfrac{\dot Q}{A}=e\,\sigma(T_b^4-T_s^4)}_{\text{radiation}}$$

In thermodynamics we usually just need the amount of heat $Q$ crossing the boundary; the rate laws tell us which direction it flows and remind us it is a path function, not a property.

The first law for a closed system

Conservation of energy says the change in a closed system's stored energy equals net heat in minus net work out:

$$\Delta U + \Delta KE + \Delta PE = Q - W \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{dE}{dt} = \dot Q - \dot W$$

The rate form on the right is the same statement per unit time. This single equation — the energy balance — is the engine of the whole course; every later result specialises it.

Cycles, efficiency and COP

A cycle returns the system to its initial state, so $\Delta E = 0$ and the net heat equals the net work, $Q_{cycle}=W_{cycle}$. That lets us define the figures of merit we will chase for the rest of the semester: thermal efficiency for a power cycle, and the coefficient of performance for refrigeration and heat-pump cycles.

$$\eta = \frac{W_{cycle}}{Q_{in}} = 1 - \frac{Q_{out}}{Q_{in}} \qquad \beta = \frac{Q_C}{W_{cycle}} \qquad \gamma = \frac{Q_H}{W_{cycle}} = \beta + 1$$
Where this leads Topic 2 supplies the property data ($u$, $h$, $v$) the energy balance needs; Topic 3 opens the system up to flow.
