1-D Steady Conduction & Thermal Resistance

The heat equation reduced to its simplest useful case: one-dimensional, steady, no generation. Solving it for plane and cylindrical walls gives temperature profiles and heat rates, and — crucially — the thermal-resistance analogy that turns conduction problems into electrical circuits.

The plane wall

With $\partial^2 T/\partial x^2 = 0$ the temperature profile is linear, and the heat rate follows from Fourier's law:

$$T(x) = T_{s,1} + (T_{s,2}-T_{s,1})\frac{x}{L}, \qquad q_x = \frac{kA}{L}(T_{s,1}-T_{s,2})$$

The thermal-resistance analogy

Writing the heat rate as (driving difference)/(resistance) exposes a direct analogy with Ohm's law. Each mode has a thermal resistance:

$$R_{t,cond} = \frac{L}{kA}, \qquad R_{t,conv} = \frac{1}{hA}, \qquad R_{t,rad} = \frac{1}{h_rA}$$

These combine in series (resistances add) and parallel exactly like electrical resistors, so a wall with fluid on each side is a chain conv → cond → conv. Convection and radiation at the same surface act in parallel, via the radiation coefficient $h_r = \varepsilon\sigma(T_s+T_{sur})(T_s^2+T_{sur}^2)$.

Composite walls & the overall coefficient

A multilayer wall collapses to a single overall heat transfer coefficient $U$ defined by $q = UA\,\Delta T$ with $1/UA = \sum R_t$. Always state the area $U$ is based on.

Worked example A car rear-window defogger: warm air at 40 °C inside ($h_i = 30$), −10 °C outside ($h_o = 65$), 4-mm glass. Summing the series resistances ($1/h_i + L/k + 1/h_o$) gives $q'' \approx 969\ \text{W/m}^2$, and the inner/outer glass surfaces come out at about 4.9 °C and 7.7 °C.

The cylindrical wall

Radial conduction through a pipe wall gives a logarithmic profile (area grows with radius), and:

$$q_r = \frac{2\pi Lk\,(T_{s,1}-T_{s,2})}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}, \qquad R_{t,cond} = \frac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{2\pi Lk}$$

A worked steam-pipe example (calcium-silicate insulation, 20 mm thick, surfaces at 800 K and 490 K on a 0.12-m pipe) gives a heat loss of about 2377 W/m of pipe.

Contact resistance & generation

Real joints are rough — only asperities touch — so a temperature jump appears across an interface, captured by the per-area contact resistance $R''_{t,c}$, added in series. When a wall generates heat internally (resistive heating, nuclear fuel), the profile becomes parabolic and the maximum temperature sits where the flux is zero — the mid-plane of a symmetric wall or the centreline of a rod.

Where this leads The resistance network extends directly to fins (Week 3) and heat exchangers (Week 7), where the convection resistance $1/hA$ is exactly the term the rest of the course works to reduce.
