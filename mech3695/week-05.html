External Convection

Flow over the outside of bodies — plates, cylinders, spheres, tube banks. Each geometry has its own empirical $Nu = C\,Re^m Pr^n$ correlation. This week is about picking the right one, evaluating properties correctly, and turning the resulting Nusselt number into a heat rate.

The five-step methodology

  1. Identify the geometry (plate / cylinder / sphere / tube bank).
  2. Evaluate fluid properties at the film temperature $T_f = (T_s+T_\infty)/2$.
  3. Compute $Re$ and check laminar vs turbulent.
  4. Decide whether a local or average coefficient is needed.
  5. Select the correlation, get $Nu$, recover $h = Nu\,k/L$.

Flat plate in parallel flow

Transition occurs at the critical $Re_{x,c} = 5\times10^5$. For an isothermal plate:

$$\overline{Nu}_L = 0.664\,Re_L^{1/2}Pr^{1/3}\ \text{(laminar)}, \qquad Nu_x = 0.0296\,Re_x^{4/5}Pr^{1/3}\ \text{(turbulent)}$$

When the plate is long enough to carry both regimes, the mixed average is $\overline{Nu}_L = (0.037\,Re_L^{4/5} - 871)Pr^{1/3}$. A constant-flux (isoflux) plate uses 0.453 and 0.0308 in place of the isothermal constants.

Worked example AISI 1010 steel plates (1 m, initially 300 °C) cooled by 20 °C air at 10 m/s: at $Re_L\approx3.3\times10^5$ the flow is laminar, $\overline{Nu}_L = 0.664\,Re_L^{1/2}Pr^{1/3}$ gives $\bar h$, and the plate loses heat at a rate that sets its cooling rate $dT/dt$ via a lumped energy balance.

Cylinders and spheres

For a cylinder in cross-flow the single-correlation Churchill–Bernstein equation spans a wide range; the Zukauskas and Hilpert forms are alternatives (correlations can disagree by ~20%). For a sphere the Whitaker correlation is used, with the neat limit $Nu_D \to 2$ for a motionless fluid (pure conduction into an infinite medium):

$$\overline{Nu}_D = 2 + \big(0.4\,Re_D^{1/2}+0.06\,Re_D^{2/3}\big)Pr^{0.4}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu_s}\right)^{1/4}$$

Falling liquid drops use the simpler Ranz–Marshall form.

Banks of tubes

For flow across a tube bank (a heat-exchanger core), the Zukauskas correlation $\overline{Nu}_D = C_2\,C_1\,Re_{D,max}^m Pr^{0.36}(Pr/Pr_s)^{1/4}$ uses the maximum Reynolds number (at the minimum flow area between tubes), a row-count factor $C_2$, and constants that depend on the aligned/staggered arrangement. The stream temperature changes through the bank, so the log-mean difference is used for the heat rate.

Where this leads The tube-bank result feeds directly into the shell-side of a heat exchanger (Week 7). Next week turns the flow inward — convection inside ducts.
