Radiation Exchange Between Surfaces

Real problems involve several surfaces exchanging radiation in an enclosure. Two ingredients are needed: the geometry — captured by the view factor — and the surface properties from Week 9. Together they build a radiation network that mirrors the thermal-resistance circuits of conduction.

The view factor

The view factor $F_{ij}$ is the fraction of radiation leaving surface $i$ that strikes surface $j$ — a purely geometric quantity from the double-integral definition. In practice it is read from charts (aligned/perpendicular rectangles, coaxial disks) or built from the algebra rules.

The view-factor rules

  • Reciprocity: $A_i F_{ij} = A_j F_{ji}$.
  • Summation: $\sum_j F_{ij} = 1$ — everything leaving lands somewhere in the enclosure (including the surface itself, if concave).
  • Superposition: a compound surface's view factor is the sum of its parts.
  • Symmetry: surfaces symmetric about a third have identical view factors from it.

Careful inspection with these four rules resolves most enclosure geometries without integrating. A flat or convex surface has $F_{ii} = 0$; a concave one sees itself.

Blackbody exchange

Black surfaces reflect nothing, so exchange depends only on temperatures and geometry:

$$q_{ij} = A_i F_{ij}\,\sigma\,(T_i^4 - T_j^4)$$

The gray-surface network

For opaque, diffuse, gray surfaces, the net radiation leaving a surface is driven across a surface resistance from its blackbody potential to its radiosity, and radiation travels between surfaces across a space resistance:

$$q_i = \frac{E_{bi}-J_i}{(1-\varepsilon_i)/\varepsilon_i A_i}, \qquad q_{ij} = \frac{J_i - J_j}{1/A_iF_{ij}}$$

This is a resistance network exactly like conduction. For the common two-surface enclosure it collapses to three resistances in series:

$$q_1 = \frac{\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)}{\dfrac{1-\varepsilon_1}{\varepsilon_1 A_1} + \dfrac{1}{A_1 F_{12}} + \dfrac{1-\varepsilon_2}{\varepsilon_2 A_2}}$$

Specialising the geometry recovers the standard results: large parallel plates, long concentric cylinders, concentric spheres, and a small body in large surroundings (which reduces to $q = \varepsilon_1 A_1\sigma(T_1^4-T_2^4)$ — the Week 1 formula). Inserting a low-emissivity radiation shield adds resistances and sharply cuts the exchange.

Worked example A semicircular drying oven: the curved wall at 1200 K radiates to a water-coated flat base at 350 K, both treated as black. With $F$ from the geometry, $q' = A' F\sigma(T_{wall}^4 - T_{base}^4)$ per unit length sets the evaporation (drying) rate through the latent heat.

Where this leads Radiation completes the three modes. Week 11 returns to conduction to solve the multidimensional and transient heat equation numerically.
