Week 9 · Lecture notes

Radiation: Processes & Properties

The third mode, and the one that needs no medium. All matter above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation. This week defines the ideal blackbody, the spectral distribution of emission, and the surface properties — emissivity, absorptivity, reflectivity, transmissivity — needed before we can compute exchange between surfaces.

The nature of thermal radiation

Radiation is energy carried by electromagnetic waves (equivalently, photons of energy $E = h\nu$) released as the electronic configuration of atoms changes. It travels at the speed of light, needs no medium, and can pass between bodies through a colder intervening one. Thermal radiation spans roughly $0.1$–$100\ \mu\text{m}$.

The blackbody

A blackbody is the ideal reference: it absorbs all incident radiation at every wavelength and direction, and no surface can emit more at a given temperature. It is a diffuse emitter, and its total emissive power is the Stefan–Boltzmann law:

$$E_b = \sigma T^4, \qquad \sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}^4$$

The spectral distribution — how that power spreads over wavelength — is the Planck distribution, and its peak shifts with temperature by Wien's law $\lambda_{max}T = 2898\ \mu\text{m}\cdot\text{K}$. Hence the Sun (≈5800 K) peaks in the visible while room-temperature surfaces emit in the infrared — the fact behind the greenhouse effect.

Emission, irradiation, radiosity

Three fluxes describe radiation at a surface: emissive power $E$ (emitted), irradiation $G$ (incident), and radiosity $J$ (all radiation leaving — emitted plus reflected). Incident radiation splits into reflected, absorbed and transmitted parts:

$$\rho + \alpha + \tau = 1$$

For an opaque surface $\tau = 0$, so $\rho + \alpha = 1$ and the net flux is $q''_{rad} = J - G$, or $q'' = \varepsilon\sigma T_s^4 - \alpha G$ combining emission and absorption.

Surface properties & idealisations

Emissivity $\varepsilon = E/E_b$ rates a real surface against a blackbody. Kirchhoff's law gives $\varepsilon = \alpha$ for a gray surface (properties independent of wavelength); a diffuse surface has properties independent of direction. These two idealisations make the enclosure analysis of Week 10 tractable.

Worked example An opaque surface at 80 °C with $E = 628\ \text{W/m}^2$, $G = 1380\ \text{W/m}^2$, $\rho = 0.30$: absorptivity $\alpha = 1-\rho = 0.70$; net radiative flux $J - G = (E + \rho G) - G = E - \alpha G = 628 - 0.7(1380) = -338\ \text{W/m}^2$ (into the surface). Adding convection at $h = 20$, $T_\infty = 25\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}$ gives a combined flux of about 762 W/m² leaving.
