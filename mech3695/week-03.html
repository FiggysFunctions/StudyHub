Extended Surfaces (Fins)

To raise the convective heat rate $q = hA_s(T_s-T_\infty)$ you can lift $h$, lift $\Delta T$, or — most practically — add area. A fin is extended material that increases the convecting area. This week derives its temperature distribution and heat rate, then the three measures of how well a fin actually performs.

The fin equation

An energy balance on a differential slice — conduction in, conduction out, convection off the sides — gives, for a uniform cross-section, the fin equation in the temperature excess $\theta = T - T_\infty$:

$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2} - m^2\theta = 0, \qquad m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}}$$

This linear ODE has the general solution $\theta = C_1 e^{mx} + C_2 e^{-mx}$ (hyperbolic functions). The fin parameter $m$ sets how quickly the fin cools toward the fluid along its length.

The four tip conditions

The base condition is always $\theta(0)=\theta_b$; the second condition is the fin tip, giving four standard cases:

  • Case A — convecting tip: the physically complete case, with a $(h/mk)$ tip term.
  • Case B — adiabatic tip: negligible tip loss, $\theta/\theta_b = \cosh m(L-x)/\cosh mL$. Tip convection is folded in later with a corrected length.
  • Case C — prescribed tip temperature.
  • Case D — infinite fin: $\theta/\theta_b = e^{-mx}$, tip reaches the fluid temperature.

The heat rate is found from Fourier's law at the base. For the adiabatic tip $q_f = M\tanh mL$ with $M = \sqrt{hPkA_c}\,\theta_b$; for the infinite fin $q_f = M$.

Three performance measures

Fin efficiency — actual heat vs. heat if the whole fin were at the base temperature:

$$\eta_f = \frac{q_f}{hA_f\theta_b} = \frac{\tanh(mL_c)}{mL_c}$$

The corrected length $L_c = L + t/2$ (rectangular) or $L + D/4$ (pin) accounts for tip convection while still using the tidy adiabatic formula, valid when $(ht/k)\lesssim0.0625$. Fin effectiveness compares the fin to the bare base it covers:

$$\varepsilon_f = \frac{q_f}{hA_{c,b}\theta_b}, \qquad \varepsilon_{f,\infty} = \sqrt{\frac{kP}{hA_c}}$$

A fin is only worth adding if $\varepsilon_f \gtrsim 2$ — favoured by high $k$, thin closely-spaced fins (large $P/A_c$) and low $h$, which is why fins live on the gas side of heat exchangers. Finally, fin resistance $R_{t,f} = \theta_b/q_f = 1/(hA_f\eta_f)$ slots the fin straight into a resistance network.

Worked example A long aluminium rod fin behaves as Case D, $q_f \sim \sqrt{k}D^{3/2}$. Tripling the diameter raises the heat removal by $3^{3/2}\approx5.2\times$; swapping aluminium ($k\approx240$) for copper ($k\approx400$) raises it by $\sqrt{400/240}\approx1.3\times$.

Fin arrays

Real heat sinks use arrays of $N$ fins on a base. The overall surface efficiency $\eta_o = 1 - (NA_f/A_t)(1-\eta_f)$ combines the finned and exposed-base area into one factor, giving $q_t = \eta_o hA_t\theta_b$ and a single array resistance. Straight triangular fins are often optimal — near-rectangular performance for much less material.

Where this leads Fins reappear in Week 7 as the dominant way to boost the gas-side $U$ of a finned-tube heat exchanger, entering through the overall surface efficiency.
