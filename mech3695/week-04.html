Introduction to Convection

So far $h$ was simply given. Now we open it up. Convection is governed by the boundary layer that forms on a surface, and the whole subject is organised by three dimensionless groups — Reynolds, Prandtl and Nusselt — that fall out of non-dimensionalising the boundary-layer equations.

The boundary layers

A boundary layer is the thin region next to a surface where the fluid adjusts from the wall condition to the free stream. The velocity boundary layer (thickness $\delta$) is where $u$ rises to 99% of $u_\infty$; the thermal boundary layer ($\delta_t$) is where temperature adjusts. At the wall the fluid is stationary (no-slip), so heat crosses that first film purely by conduction:

$$h = \frac{-k_f\,\partial T/\partial y\big|_{y=0}}{T_s - T_\infty}$$

This is the key insight: $h$ is set by the temperature gradient in the fluid at the wall. As the layer thickens downstream the gradient — and hence the local $h$ — decreases.

Local vs average coefficients

Because $h$ varies along a surface, we distinguish the local $h(x)$ from the surface average $\bar h = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L h\,dx$. The average sets the total heat rate $q = \bar h A(T_s - T_\infty)$; the local value gives the flux at a point.

The dimensionless groups

Non-dimensionalising the continuity, momentum and energy equations for the boundary layer produces three groups that every correlation is written in:

$$Re_L = \frac{u_\infty L}{\nu}, \qquad Pr = \frac{\nu}{\alpha}, \qquad Nu = \frac{hL}{k}$$

The Reynolds number (inertia/viscosity) decides laminar vs turbulent flow. The Prandtl number is a fluid property comparing momentum and thermal diffusion — it sets the relative thickness of the two boundary layers. The Nusselt number is the dimensionless $h$. Convection theory reduces to one statement:

$$Nu = f(Re, Pr)$$

Every correlation in Weeks 5–6 is a specific form of this function, from which $h = Nu\,k/L$ is recovered. Momentum and heat transfer are analogous, so the friction coefficient $C_f$ and $Nu$ are closely related.

Two routes to Nu Theoretical — solve the boundary-layer equations (possible only for simple geometries). Empirical — correlate experiments as $Nu = C\,Re^m Pr^n$. Engineering practice, and this course, use the empirical route, evaluating fluid properties at the film temperature.
Where this leads Week 5 applies this to external flows (flat plate, cylinder, sphere); Week 6 to internal pipe flow, where the "free stream" is replaced by a mean temperature that changes along the duct.
