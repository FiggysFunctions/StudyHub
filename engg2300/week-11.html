Multiphase Flow

Everything so far assumed one phase filling the pipe. Industry rarely obliges — oil and gas share a pipeline, steam and water share a boiler tube, slurries carry solids, and bubble columns and fluidised beds are built around the interaction. When two phases flow together the geometry they adopt becomes as important as the flow rates.

Describing a two-phase flow

Single-phase flow needs one velocity. Two-phase flow needs to say how much of each phase there is and how the pipe is shared between them:

$$x = \frac{\dot m_G}{\dot m_G + \dot m_L} \qquad \alpha = \frac{A_G}{A} \qquad H_L = 1-\alpha$$
  • Quality $x$ — the mass fraction that is gas.
  • Void fraction $\alpha$ — the fraction of the pipe cross-section occupied by gas.
  • Liquid holdup $H_L$ — what remains, $1-\alpha$.
Quality is not void fraction They are different quantities in different units, and they are not simply related — because the phases do not travel at the same speed. Gas is lighter and typically moves faster, so it occupies less of the pipe than its volumetric share would suggest. That difference is called slip.

Superficial velocities

Because we usually know the flow rates but not the in-situ areas, it is convenient to define the velocity each phase would have if it flowed alone through the whole pipe:

$$u_{SG} = \frac{Q_G}{A} \qquad u_{SL} = \frac{Q_L}{A} \qquad u_G = \frac{u_{SG}}{\alpha},\quad u_L = \frac{u_{SL}}{1-\alpha}$$

The superficial velocity is not a real velocity — each phase only has part of the area, so the actual velocity is higher. But superficial velocities can be computed straight from measured flow rates, which is why they form the axes of every flow-regime map.

Flow regimes

The two phases arrange themselves into recognisable patterns, and which one you get depends on the flow rates, the pipe orientation and the fluid properties.

Vertical upward flow, as the gas rate increases:

  • Bubble — discrete small bubbles dispersed through a continuous liquid.
  • Slug — bubbles coalesce into large bullet-shaped "Taylor" bubbles nearly filling the pipe, separated by liquid slugs.
  • Churn — the slugs break down into a chaotic, oscillating mess.
  • Annular — liquid is driven to the wall as a film with a fast gas core up the middle, often carrying entrained droplets.

Horizontal flow adds gravity acting across the pipe rather than along it, so density difference separates the phases: stratified (smooth layers), wavy, plug and slug, before also reaching annular at high gas rates.

Flow-regime maps and why they matter

Plotting superficial gas velocity against superficial liquid velocity divides the plane into regions, one per regime. Given the mass flow rate and quality you compute the two superficial velocities, plot the point, and read off the regime.

This is the first step of any two-phase calculation, because the regime controls almost everything downstream of it:

  • Pressure drop — annular and slug flow lose pressure very differently.
  • Heat transfer — a wall wetted by liquid film behaves nothing like one exposed to gas.
  • Stability and mechanical loading — slug flow delivers large intermittent momentum loads, which is a genuine design problem for pipelines and their supports.
Where this leads Week 12 closes the course with the numerical methods used when the flow is too complex for any of the analytical treatments so far.
