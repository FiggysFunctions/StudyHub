Boundary Layers, Lift & Drag

External flow — around aerofoils, vehicles, buildings and bridges. The fluid force on a body splits into drag along the flow and lift across it, and both are dominated by what the thin viscous layer next to the surface is doing. Note the key result up front: if viscosity were zero, the drag would be zero too.

The boundary layer

Because of the no-slip condition, the fluid at the surface is stationary while the free stream far away is not. The boundary layer is the thin region bridging the two, conventionally ending where the velocity reaches 99% of the free stream. It thickens along the surface, starting laminar and — if the body is long enough or the Reynolds number high enough — transitioning to turbulent.

Outside this layer viscous effects are negligible and the flow behaves as if inviscid, which is what makes Bernoulli usable there. Inside it, viscosity is everything.

Resolving the force

Integrating pressure and shear stress over the whole surface gives a single resultant force, which we resolve relative to the oncoming flow:

  • Drag — the component parallel to the flow.
  • Lift — the component perpendicular to it.

A symmetric body at zero angle of attack produces no lift; tilting it or cambering it generates the pressure asymmetry that does.

Drag itself has two sources. Friction (skin) drag is the integrated wall shear stress. Pressure (form) drag comes from the pressure being lower behind the body than in front. Which dominates depends entirely on whether the flow separates.

Separation — the thing that matters most

Past the widest point of a body the pressure rises in the flow direction. This adverse pressure gradient decelerates the already-slow fluid near the wall until it reverses and the boundary layer detaches, leaving a broad low-pressure wake. That wake is what produces large pressure drag.

The counter-intuitive result A turbulent boundary layer carries more momentum near the wall, so it resists the adverse gradient longer and separates further back. It has more skin friction but a much smaller wake — and for a bluff body the wake dominates, so tripping the layer turbulent reduces total drag. That is precisely what golf-ball dimples do.

The other lever is streamlining: a gradual taper keeps the pressure gradient gentle so the flow stays attached. A streamlined strut can produce the same drag as a circular cylinder many times thinner.

Drag and lift coefficients

Rather than tabulate forces for every size and speed, non-dimensionalise by the dynamic pressure $q = \tfrac12\rho u^2$ — the kinetic energy per unit volume of the oncoming stream — and a reference area:

$$F_D = C_D\,\tfrac12\rho u^2 A \qquad F_L = C_L\,\tfrac12\rho u^2 A$$

Both forces scale with the square of velocity, which is why drag rises so steeply with speed. The reference area is conventionally the frontal area for drag and the planform area for lift — using the wrong one is a common slip. The coefficients generally depend on Reynolds number and on shape, and are read from charts or measured in a tunnel.

A standard application is terminal velocity: a falling body accelerates until drag plus buoyancy balances weight, after which it descends at constant speed.
