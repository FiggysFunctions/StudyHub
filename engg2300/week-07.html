Week 7 · Lecture notes

Viscous Flow in Pipes

Internal flow — bounded by solid surfaces on all sides. This is where the $h_{loss}$ term from Week 4 finally gets a formula, and it is probably the most-used calculation in the whole subject: sizing a pipe, choosing a pump, or working out why the pressure at the far end of a network is too low.

Entrance region and fully developed flow

Fluid enters a pipe with a roughly uniform velocity. Boundary layers grow from the wall inward, and once they meet at the centreline the profile stops changing with distance — the flow is fully developed. The length taken to get there is the entry length.

This matters because the friction factor is only constant in the fully developed region, which is what the standard loss formula assumes. In laminar flow the developed profile is the parabola from Week 5; in turbulent flow it is much blunter, because mixing evens out the momentum across the section.

Major losses — Darcy–Weisbach

The head lost to wall friction along a straight run:

$$h_f = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{u^2}{2g} \qquad \Delta P = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{\rho u^2}{2}$$

Note the dependences: loss grows linearly with length, inversely with diameter, and with the square of velocity. Since $u = Q/A \propto 1/D^2$, the loss actually scales as $D^{-5}$ at fixed flow rate — which is why going up one pipe size can cut pumping costs dramatically.

The friction factor

Everything hinges on $f$, and it behaves quite differently in the two regimes.

Laminar ($Re \lesssim 2300$) — derivable exactly from Hagen–Poiseuille:

$$f = \frac{64}{Re}$$

Roughness is irrelevant here: the wall irregularities sit inside the slow-moving fluid and never disturb the flow.

Turbulent — now roughness matters, and $f$ depends on both $Re$ and the relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$, through the Colebrook correlation:

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt f} = -2\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{Re\sqrt f}\right)$$

This is implicit — $f$ appears on both sides — so it is solved by iteration, by an explicit approximation, or simply read off the Moody chart, which plots exactly this relationship. At high Reynolds number the curves flatten into the "fully rough" regime where $f$ depends on roughness alone.

Always compute Re first The two regimes use entirely different formulas, and roughness only matters in one of them. Getting the regime wrong makes everything after it wrong.

Minor losses

Every bend, valve, elbow, sudden expansion or contraction, entrance and exit costs additional head, because the flow separates and re-forms rather than simply rubbing along a wall. Each is given a loss coefficient $K$ from tables:

$$h_L = K\,\frac{u^2}{2g} \qquad L_{eq} = \frac{KD}{f}$$

The second form re-expresses a fitting as the length of straight pipe that would cause the same loss, which is convenient when totalling up a system.

"Minor" is a misnomer In a long pipeline the friction losses dominate and the name fits. But in a short, fitting-heavy run — a pump manifold, say — the minor losses can easily exceed the major ones. Add them up; never assume they are negligible.

The total loss for a system is then simply the sum: $h_{loss} = \sum h_f + \sum h_L$, which feeds straight back into the extended Bernoulli equation of Week 4.
