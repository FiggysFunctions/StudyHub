Continuity & the Bernoulli Equation

The fluid starts moving. Conservation of mass, momentum and energy still apply, but they are written for a system — a fixed lump of matter — and in fluids we would rather watch a fixed region of space. The Reynolds transport theorem makes that translation, and out of it fall continuity, the momentum equation, and Bernoulli.

Classifying flows

  • Steady or unsteady — does anything at a fixed point change with time? Most of this course assumes steady.
  • Laminar or turbulent — orderly layers, or chaotic mixing. Decided by the Reynolds number.
  • Viscous or inviscid — is friction being modelled or idealised away?
  • Compressible or incompressible — liquids are effectively incompressible; so are gases below about Ma 0.3.
$$Re = \frac{\rho u D}{\mu} = \frac{uD}{\nu}$$

The Reynolds number is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. Low $Re$: viscous forces dominate and the flow is laminar. High $Re$: inertia dominates and it is turbulent. In pipes the transition sits near $Re \approx 2300$.

Streamlines and streamtubes

A streamline is a curve everywhere tangent to the velocity vector at an instant. Since the velocity is parallel to it by definition, no fluid can cross a streamline. Bundle a family of them and you get a streamtube, which behaves like a pipe with imaginary walls — the object Bernoulli's equation is applied along.

The Reynolds transport theorem

Physical laws (mass is conserved, $F = ma$) are statements about a system. The Reynolds transport theorem converts each into a statement about a control volume: the rate of change of a property for the system equals the rate of change stored inside the control volume plus the net rate it is carried out across the control surface. Apply it with B = mass and you get continuity; with B = momentum you get the momentum equation.

Conservation of mass

$$\dot m = \rho A u = \text{const} \qquad \rho_1A_1u_1 = \rho_2A_2u_2 \qquad \xrightarrow{\text{incompressible}} \qquad A_1u_1 = A_2u_2$$

For steady flow through a streamtube, what goes in must come out. If the density is also constant it cancels, leaving the volumetric flow rate constant — so a pipe that narrows must speed the flow up. That single fact drives every flow meter in Week 4.

The momentum equation

Newton's second law says force equals the rate of change of momentum. For a control volume at steady state:

$$\sum \vec F = \dot m\,(\vec u_{out} - \vec u_{in})$$

This is what gives the force on a pipe bend, the thrust of a jet, or the load on a turbine blade. Two cautions: it is a vector equation, so resolve into components and mind the signs; and the forces include pressure forces on the control surface as well as any reaction holding the object in place.

The Bernoulli equation

Apply energy conservation along a streamline with no friction, no shaft work, no heat transfer and no change in internal energy, and the result is:

$$\frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{u^2}{2g} + z = \text{const} \qquad\text{or}\qquad P + \tfrac12\rho u^2 + \rho g z = \text{const}$$

In head form the three terms are the pressure head, the velocity head and the elevation head, and their sum — the total head — is constant along the streamline. Speed the flow up and the pressure must fall; that is the whole basis of a venturi, an aerofoil and a carburettor.

Check the conditions every time Bernoulli requires steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a single streamline, with no pump or turbine in between. Real flows violate the friction condition immediately, which is exactly why Week 4 has to extend it.
Where this leads Week 4 uses Bernoulli to measure flow rate, then patches it for friction, pumps and turbines.
