Dimensional Analysis & Similarity

Because the Navier–Stokes equations rarely yield to analysis, much of fluid mechanics rests on experiment. Dimensional analysis is what makes that affordable: it collapses a long list of variables into a handful of dimensionless groups, turning an impossible test programme into a manageable one — and it is why a model in a towing tank can predict a full-size ship.

Dimensions and units

A dimension is the physical nature of a quantity; a unit is the arbitrary yardstick we measure it in. Everything in this course reduces to the primary dimensions mass $M$, length $L$, time $T$ and temperature $\Theta$. So for instance dynamic viscosity is $[ML^{-1}T^{-1}]$ and kinematic viscosity $[L^2T^{-1}]$.

Any correct physical equation is dimensionally homogeneous — every additive term carries the same dimensions. Checking this is the fastest way to catch an algebra error.

The Buckingham Pi theorem

If a problem involves $k$ dimensional variables spanning $r$ primary dimensions, it can be rewritten in terms of $k-r$ dimensionless groups:

$$\Pi_1 = \phi(\Pi_2,\ \dots,\ \Pi_{k-r})$$

The procedure the course follows:

  1. List the variables involved and count them — that is $k$.
  2. Write each in primary dimensions, and count how many distinct dimensions appear — that is $r$.
  3. Determine the number of groups, $k-r$.
  4. Choose $r$ repeating variables that between them cover every dimension. Never choose the dependent variable, and a good default for fluid problems is a length, a density and a velocity ($D$, $\rho$, $u$).
  5. Combine each remaining variable with the repeating ones raised to unknown powers, and solve for the powers that make the group dimensionless.
  6. Check that every group really is dimensionless.
Why it pays Suppose pressure drop depends on five variables. Testing ten values of each means 10⁵ experiments. Reduced to two dimensionless groups, ten values of one group against the other is 10 — and the result is universal rather than specific to your rig.

The common dimensionless groups

Each compares inertia with a different competing effect, and each governs a different class of problem:

$$Re = \frac{\rho u L}{\mu} \qquad Fr = \frac{u}{\sqrt{gL}} \qquad Ma = \frac{u}{c} \qquad We = \frac{\rho u^2 L}{\sigma}$$
  • Reynolds — inertia vs viscosity. Governs pipe flow and most internal/submerged flows.
  • Froude — inertia vs gravity. Governs anything with a free surface: ship hulls, spillways, open channels.
  • Mach — speed vs the speed of sound. Governs compressible flow.
  • Weber — inertia vs surface tension. Governs droplets, sprays and bubbles.

Similarity and scale models

Three conditions, each building on the last:

  • Geometric similarity — model and prototype are the same shape, all lengths scaled by one factor (including roughness).
  • Kinematic similarity — the velocity fields have the same shape, so streamline patterns match.
  • Dynamic similarity — the force ratios match, which requires the governing dimensionless group to be equal in both.
$$\Pi_{i,\text{model}} = \Pi_{i,\text{prototype}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\rho_m u_m L_m}{\mu_m} = \frac{\rho_p u_p L_p}{\mu_p}$$

Match the group and any dimensionless result measured on the model — a drag coefficient, a pressure coefficient — applies directly to the prototype. That is how a 3 m model in a towing tank stands in for a destroyer.

The practical difficulty Matching Reynolds number on a small model demands a very high velocity, and some problems (a ship, which has both a free surface and viscous drag) need Froude and Reynolds matched at once — which is generally impossible in the same fluid. Real testing splits the effects and recombines them.
