Pressure Distribution in a Fluid

Fluid mechanics splits into statics — fluids at rest — and dynamics. Statics is the easier half and comes first, but it is not a toy: dams, manometers, hydraulic jacks and ship stability all sit here. This week sets up the properties we will use all semester, then works out how pressure varies through a fluid at rest.

Fluid properties

A fluid deforms continuously under shear — liquids (molecules close but free to slide) and gases (molecules far apart and moving freely). Properties are intensive if they do not depend on how much substance there is (pressure, temperature) and extensive if they do (mass, volume).

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V} \qquad SG = \frac{\rho}{\rho_{H_2O}} \qquad \gamma_s = \rho g$$

Density is mass per unit volume; specific gravity compares it to water at 4 °C and so is dimensionless; specific weight is the weight of a unit volume.

Viscosity

Viscosity measures a fluid's resistance to flow, and comes from internal friction between layers forced to move relative to one another:

$$\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$$

Dynamic viscosity $\mu$ is in Pa·s (or kg/m·s); kinematic viscosity $\nu$ is in m²/s (1 stoke = 1 cm²/s). Setting $\mu = 0$ gives an inviscid flow — a useful fiction with no friction at the wall. Every real flow is viscous, which means it obeys the no-slip condition: the fluid touching a surface moves with that surface, and the velocity builds up as you move away from it.

Pressure

Pressure is a normal force per unit area — a fluid always pushes perpendicular to a boundary, never along it.

$$P = \frac{F_n}{A} \qquad [\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2]$$

Other units you will meet: 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa, 1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg, 1 psi ≈ 6.9 kPa. Pressure is nearly always quoted against a reference:

$$P_{abs} = P_{atm} + P_{gauge} \qquad P_{gauge} = P - P_{atm}$$
Gauge or absolute? A negative gauge pressure is a partial vacuum. Gauge is what a pressure gauge reads and what most of this course uses; absolute is what the gas laws need. Getting them confused is the classic error.

The hydrostatic equation

Take a cylinder of stationary fluid of area $A$ and height $h$. It is at rest, so the forces balance: the pressure on the bottom must support the pressure on the top plus the weight of the column, $\rho A h g$. Dividing through by $A$:

$$P_1 = P_2 + \rho g h \qquad \Delta P = \rho g h$$

Rearranged as $h = \Delta P/\rho g$, the height is called the pressure head — a way of expressing pressure as a column of fluid that makes every term in Bernoulli's equation later share the same units.

Two consequences worth internalising Pressure in a connected, homogeneous, stationary fluid depends only on depth — so it is the same at every point on a given horizontal level, and it does not depend on the shape or width of the container. A tall thin tube and a wide reservoir at the same depth read the same pressure.

Pascal's law and the hydraulic press

Pascal's law: pressure applied to a confined fluid at rest is transmitted equally in every direction, and at a point the pressure is the same on every plane through it ($P_x = P_y = P_s$). The hydraulic press turns that into force multiplication:

$$P_1 = P_2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} \;\Rightarrow\; F_2 = F_1\frac{A_2}{A_1}$$

A small force on a small piston produces a large force on a large one, in proportion to the area ratio — the principle behind car jacks and hydraulic brakes.

Measuring pressure

A barometer is a column of liquid with atmosphere on one side and a vacuum on the other, so $P_{atm} = \rho g h$. Mercury is used because its high density keeps the column manageable (about 760 mm rather than 10 m of water) and its vapour pressure is negligible, so the "vacuum" really is one.

A U-tube manometer measures a pressure difference by the height a denser fluid is displaced. Work along the tube from one end to the other, adding $\rho g h$ whenever you descend and subtracting it whenever you rise:

$$P_A + \rho_1 g h_1 - \rho_{man} g h_2 = P_B$$
Where this leads Week 2 integrates this pressure distribution over submerged surfaces to get the forces on gates, dams and floating bodies.
