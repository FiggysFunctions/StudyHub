Pipe Systems 1

Everything so far assembles here. The extended Bernoulli equation supplies the framework, Week 7 supplies the losses, and the result is the analysis of a real installation — a reservoir, a pump, a run of pipe with fittings, and a delivery point.

Energy and hydraulic grade lines

A useful way to picture where head is going along a system:

$$EGL = \frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{u^2}{2g} + z \qquad HGL = \frac{P}{\rho g} + z = EGL - \frac{u^2}{2g}$$

The EGL is the total head; the HGL sits one velocity head below it. Reading a system left to right:

  • At a reservoir surface both lines meet the water level (the velocity is negligible and the pressure atmospheric).
  • Along a pipe the EGL slopes steadily downward — that slope is the friction loss.
  • At a fitting the EGL steps down; at a pump it jumps up; at a turbine it drops.
Reading a warning off the diagram Where the HGL falls below the pipe, the pressure there is below atmospheric. Take it far enough below and the liquid reaches its vapour pressure and cavitates — which is destructive, and why pump suction lines are designed carefully.

Single-pipe systems

Write the extended Bernoulli equation between two convenient points — usually two reservoir surfaces, where the pressure and velocity terms vanish and only elevation remains:

$$z_1 + h_{pump} = z_2 + h_{turbine} + \sum h_f + \sum h_L$$

Everything then comes down to which quantity is unknown. The three classic problem types:

  1. Find the head loss, given flow rate and geometry. Direct: compute $Re$, get $f$, apply Darcy–Weisbach.
  2. Find the flow rate, given the available head. Iterative, because $f$ depends on $Re$ which depends on the very velocity you are solving for. Guess $f$ (0.02 is a reasonable start), solve for $u$, recompute $Re$ and $f$, repeat — it converges in two or three passes.
  3. Find the diameter, given flow and allowable head loss. Also iterative, and usually finished by rounding up to the next commercially available size.

Pipes in series and parallel

$$\text{Series: } Q = Q_1 = Q_2,\ \ h_L = h_{L1}+h_{L2} \qquad \text{Parallel: } h_L = h_{L1} = h_{L2},\ \ Q = Q_1+Q_2$$

In series the same flow passes through every pipe, so the losses accumulate. In parallel each branch spans the same two junctions and therefore suffers the same head loss — so the flow distributes itself between branches precisely so as to equalise it. The larger, smoother or shorter branch takes more.

This is a direct analogue of resistors in a circuit, and the same intuition carries over.

Pumps in a system

A pump does not deliver a fixed flow. Its performance curve — head against flow rate, supplied by the manufacturer along with efficiency and power — falls as flow increases. The system curve is the head the pipework demands, a static lift plus losses growing roughly as $Q^2$. The pump settles where the two cross: the operating point.

Reading across from it gives the delivered flow, the head, the efficiency and hence the shaft power:

$$\dot W_{pump} = \frac{\rho g Q h_{pump}}{\eta_{pump}} \qquad \dot W_{turbine} = \eta_{turbine}\,\rho g Q h_{turbine}$$

Turbines are analysed the same way, with the sign of the head term reversed and the efficiency multiplying rather than dividing.

Where this leads Week 10 takes on systems where the equations can no longer be solved directly — branched networks and loops needing iteration.
