Turbulent & Wall-Bounded Flows

Turbulent variables fluctuate randomly in space and time, and no exact solution is known. But engineers rarely need the instantaneous field — we need means. Reynolds' time-averaging delivers exactly that, at the cost of introducing new unknowns, and it is the idea every turbulence model in CFD is built on.

Wall-bounded flows

Flows bounded by at least one wall — pipes, channels and boundary layers — as distinct from free shear flows like jets and wakes. The wall imposes the no-slip condition, forcing a viscosity-dominated region right next to it. That creates two length scales: a small viscous one near the wall and a large one for the flow as a whole, and reconciling them is what the layer structure below is about.

Reynolds' time-averaging

Split every quantity into a time mean plus a fluctuation, averaging over a period longer than any significant fluctuation:

$$u = \bar u + u' \qquad \bar u = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T u\,dt \qquad \overline{u'} = 0, \quad \overline{u'^2} \neq 0$$

The fluctuation averages to zero by construction — but its mean square does not, and that is the measure of turbulence intensity. Substituting into the momentum equation and averaging leaves continuity looking unchanged but momentum carrying extra terms:

$$\tau = \mu\frac{d\bar u}{dy} - \rho\,\overline{u'v'}$$

The new term $-\rho\overline{u'v'}$ is the Reynolds stress, and across most of a turbulent flow it dwarfs the viscous stress. It is also unknown — the closure problem. Modelling it with an eddy viscosity $\mu_t$ or Prandtl's mixing length is what "turbulence modelling" means, and it is the reason CFD asks you to pick a model.

The law of the wall

The wall shear stress defines a velocity scale, and with it a set of dimensionless wall coordinates:

$$u^* = \sqrt{\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}} \qquad u^+ = \frac{u}{u^*} \qquad y^+ = \frac{y\,u^*}{\nu}$$

In these variables, turbulent wall data from every flow collapse onto a single curve with three regions:

  • Viscous sublayer ($y^+ < 5$) — viscosity dominates and the profile is linear: $u^+ = y^+$.
  • Buffer layer ($5 < y^+ < 30$) — the curve bends over to merge with the log law.
  • Logarithmic overlap layer — $u^+ = \frac{1}{\kappa}\ln y^+ + B$, with $\kappa \approx 0.41$ and $B \approx 5.0$ for a smooth wall.

Further out sits the outer velocity-defect layer. This structure — Prandtl 1930, von Kármán 1933, Millikan 1937 — is described in the lecture as the approximation that solves nearly every turbulent-flow problem.

Solving the log law Because $u^*$ appears on both sides, finding it from a measured velocity means solving an implicit equation. The lecture recommends Excel's Goal Seek or Solver — worth practising before Quiz 2, which asks for exactly this.

Turbulent pipe flow

A force balance on a cylindrical plug of fluid links wall shear to the pressure gradient, $\tau_w\,2\pi r L = \Delta p\,\pi r^2$, and the head loss follows from the Darcy friction factor (Moody chart or a correlation):

$$\tau_w = \frac{\Delta p\,R}{2L} \qquad h_f = f\frac{L}{D}\frac{V^2}{2g}$$

Integrating the log law across the pipe relates the mean velocity to the centreline value — and shows the turbulent profile is much flatter than the laminar parabola, since vigorous mixing evens out the momentum. The same treatment applies between parallel plates using the hydraulic diameter $D_h = 4h$.

The flat-plate boundary layer

The boundary layer is where $u$ reaches 99% of the free-stream value. Its growth depends entirely on the regime:

$$\frac{\delta}{x} \approx \frac{5.0}{Re_x^{1/2}} \ \ \text{(laminar, Blasius)} \qquad \frac{\delta}{x} \approx \frac{0.16}{Re_x^{1/7}} \ \ \text{(turbulent)}$$
Always check the regime The worked example does this explicitly: assume laminar, compute $x$, then check $Re_x$. Getting $9\times10^8$ against a transition value near $5\times10^5$ means the assumption was wrong — redo it with the turbulent relation. Marks are lost by skipping this check.

Momentum and displacement thickness

Kármán's momentum integral gives the drag directly from the velocity profile:

$$\theta = \int_0^\delta \frac{u}{U}\left(1-\frac{u}{U}\right)dy \qquad \delta^* = \int_0^\delta\left(1-\frac{u}{U}\right)dy \qquad D = \rho b U^2\theta$$

The displacement thickness is how far the outer streamlines must deflect outward to conserve mass past the retarded near-wall flow. Because $\delta^* \ll x$ at high Reynolds number, the wall-normal velocity is far smaller than the streamwise one — the key assumption underpinning boundary-layer theory. Their ratio $H = \delta^*/\theta$ is the shape factor. Skin friction and drag then follow as coefficients:

$$c_f = \frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U^2} \qquad C_D = \frac{D}{\tfrac12\rho U^2 bL}$$

There is no exact solution for turbulent flat-plate flow; the standard result comes from assuming the log law holds all the way across the layer, plus power-law and curve-fit approximations for the rough and transitional regimes.

Where this leads The Reynolds stresses are exactly what a CFD turbulence model has to supply, and $y^+$ is what decides whether your mesh resolves the near-wall region — both of which return in Week 7.
