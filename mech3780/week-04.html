Compressible Flow

Everything before this assumed the fluid velocity was far below its speed of sound. Once $Ma \gtrsim 0.3$ density changes stop being negligible, and with them come significant temperature and pressure changes — so the energy equation can no longer be ignored. The work doubles from two equations to four.

Four equations, four unknowns

Compressible flow requires continuity, momentum, energy and the equation of state, solved simultaneously for $p$, $\rho$, $T$ and $V$. The general theory is complicated, so we simplify hard — usually by assuming reversible adiabatic (isentropic) flow.

Continuity changes Incompressible: $A_1V_1 = A_2V_2$. Compressible: $\rho_1A_1V_1 = \rho_2A_2V_2$. Dropping the density is exactly the approximation that fails here.

The thermodynamic toolkit

$$p = \rho R T \qquad h = u + \frac{p}{\rho} \qquad c_p - c_v = R \qquad k = \frac{c_p}{c_v} \qquad c_p = \frac{kR}{k-1}$$

with $du = c_v dT$ and $dh = c_p dT$ for a perfect gas. For air $R = 287$ J/kg·K and $k = 1.4$. Entropy comes from the Gibbs relation $T\,ds = dh - dp/\rho$, integrating to:

$$s_2 - s_1 = c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}$$

An isentropic process is adiabatic and reversible, giving $p/\rho^k = \text{const}$. Note carefully: all isentropic processes are adiabatic, but not all adiabatic processes are isentropic — the normal shock is the counterexample that matters.

Speed of sound and the Mach number

Applying continuity and momentum across a weak pressure pulse (with friction confined inside the wave) gives the propagation speed as a thermodynamic property:

$$a = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!s}} = \sqrt{kRT} \qquad Ma = \frac{V}{a}$$

For air at 20 °C, $a \approx 340$ m/s — so a shout carries 34 m in about a tenth of a second. The Mach number classifies everything: $Ma<0.3$ effectively incompressible, $0.3<Ma<1$ subsonic compressible, $Ma\approx1$ transonic, $Ma>1$ supersonic, $3<Ma<5$ high supersonic and beyond that hypersonic.

Stagnation and sonic states

For adiabatic flow past an insulated wall with no shaft work, the steady-flow energy equation gives a constant along every streamtube — the stagnation enthalpy, the value the fluid would reach if brought to rest:

$$h_0 = h + \tfrac12 V^2 \qquad \frac{T_0}{T} = 1 + \frac{k-1}{2}Ma^2 \qquad \frac{p_0}{p} = \left(1 + \frac{k-1}{2}Ma^2\right)^{\!k/(k-1)}$$
Which relation needs what The temperature ratio needs only adiabatic flow. The pressure and density ratios additionally require isentropic flow. That distinction is precisely why $T_0$ survives a shock but $p_0$ does not.

The sonic (critical) state where $Ma=1$ is the other useful reference, marked with an asterisk:

$$\frac{T^*}{T_0} = \frac{2}{k+1} \qquad \frac{p^*}{p_0} = \left(\frac{2}{k+1}\right)^{\!k/(k-1)} \approx 0.528\ (k=1.4)$$

Isentropic flow with area change

Combining continuity and the Bernoulli-type momentum relation for isentropic duct flow yields the central result:

$$\frac{dV}{V} = -\frac{dA}{A}\,\frac{1}{1 - Ma^2}$$

The sign of $(1-Ma^2)$ flips everything. Subsonically a contraction accelerates the flow, as intuition expects; supersonically an expansion accelerates it. To pass smoothly from subsonic to supersonic you therefore need a throat at $Ma=1$ followed by a diverging section — the converging-diverging (de Laval) nozzle. The area–Mach relation gives the required contour, and once the throat reaches sonic conditions the duct is choked: lowering the back pressure further cannot increase the mass flow.

Friction and heat transfer

Two non-isentropic duct flows worth knowing by name. Fanno flow is adiabatic with wall friction: friction drives subsonic flow up toward $Ma=1$ and supersonic flow down toward it. Rayleigh flow is frictionless with heat addition or removal; because it is not adiabatic, the stagnation temperature is no longer constant. In both, properties at any station follow from the known Mach number relative to the critical reference.

Normal shock waves

When supersonic flow meets an abrupt area decrease or too high a back pressure, a normal shock forms — a region only a few micrometres thick across which properties jump. The flow goes supersonic → subsonic; pressure, temperature and density rise; velocity and Mach number fall.

$$Ma_2^2 = \frac{1 + \tfrac12(k-1)Ma_1^2}{k\,Ma_1^2 - \tfrac12(k-1)} \qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1} = \frac{2k\,Ma_1^2 - (k-1)}{k+1}$$

The process is adiabatic but irreversible, so entropy increases and stagnation pressure is lost. That means the incompressible Bernoulli equation must never be applied across a shock. In a converging-diverging nozzle with insufficient pressure ratio, the supersonic section cannot expand fully and a shock appears in the diverging part.
