What You Need From ENGG2300

MECH3780 assumes ENGG2300 and does not re-derive it. This page is the bridge: the prerequisite material condensed to what actually gets used, with each section saying where in this course it comes back. If a topic here feels shaky, that is the one to go back and read properly — the link at the end of each section goes straight to the full treatment.

1 · Fluid properties and the two viscosities

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V} \qquad \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$$

Density you will use everywhere. The thing to keep straight is the two viscosities: dynamic $\mu$ in Pa·s appears in stress laws, and kinematic $\nu = \mu/\rho$ in m²/s appears in the Reynolds number and in $y^+$. Mixing them up is the single most common unit error in this course.

Comes back in Week 5, where the wall coordinate $y^+ = yu^*/\nu$ uses the kinematic one, and the Reynolds stress uses the dynamic one. ENGG2300 Week 1 →

2 · Pressure, head and hydrostatics

$$\Delta P = \rho g h \qquad h = \frac{\Delta P}{\rho g} \qquad P_{abs} = P_{atm} + P_{gauge}$$

Expressing pressure as a head — an equivalent height of fluid — is what makes every term in the energy equation share units, and it is how pump performance is quoted. Keep gauge and absolute distinct: pump curves are in head (gauge-ish), but cavitation calculations need absolute pressure against the vapour pressure.

Comes back in Weeks 1–2, in net head, system curves and NPSH. ENGG2300 Week 1 →

3 · Continuity and the momentum theorem

$$\dot m = \rho A V \qquad A_1V_1 = A_2V_2 \qquad \sum \vec F = \dot m(\vec V_{out} - \vec V_{in})$$

Continuity is the workhorse: a narrowing passage speeds the flow up. Note the incompressible form drops $\rho$ — legitimate for pumps and water, not for the compressible flow of Week 4, where changing density is the entire point.

The momentum equation is a vector statement, and it is the ancestor of two results in this course: the force of a jet on a turbine bucket, and — in angular form, taking moments about the shaft — the Euler turbomachine equation.

Comes back in Week 1 (Euler equation), Week 2 (jet force on a bucket), Week 4 (compressible continuity). ENGG2300 Week 3 →

4 · The extended Bernoulli equation

$$\frac{p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 + h_{pump} = \frac{p_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 + h_{turbine} + h_{loss}$$

Probably the most important single equation to bring with you. Plain Bernoulli requires inviscid flow with no machine in the loop — useless for a real pump circuit. The extended form adds the three terms that matter, with the signs worth stating out loud: $h_{loss}$ is always positive (friction never gives head back), a pump adds head, a turbine extracts it.

Comes back in Week 1 — the pump's net head is precisely the difference this equation measures across the machine. ENGG2300 Week 4 →

5 · Power and efficiency

$$P_w = \rho g Q H \qquad \eta = \frac{P_w}{bhp}$$

The hydraulic power delivered to (or taken from) the fluid is $\rho g Q H$. A pump must be supplied more shaft power than that, so you divide by efficiency; a turbine delivers less, so you multiply. Getting that the wrong way round is the classic slip, and it is easy to spot — if your pump appears to need less power than the fluid gained, you have inverted it.

Comes back in Week 1, where the single efficiency splits into volumetric, hydraulic and mechanical components. ENGG2300 Week 4 →

6 · Reynolds number and flow regime

$$Re = \frac{\rho V D}{\mu} = \frac{VD}{\nu} \qquad Re_D \gtrsim 2300 \Rightarrow \text{turbulent}$$

Inertia over viscosity, and the first thing to compute in almost every problem. It decides whether you are in the laminar or turbulent branch of the friction factor, whether a boundary layer separates early or late, and whether a turbulence model is needed at all in a simulation.

Comes back in Everywhere — Week 5's entire wall treatment, and Week 7 where it is the first check before setting up a CFD case. ENGG2300 Week 3 →

7 · Pipe losses

$$h_f = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{V^2}{2g} \qquad f = \frac{64}{Re}\ (\text{laminar}) \qquad h_L = K\frac{V^2}{2g}\ (\text{minor})$$

Darcy–Weisbach for the straight runs, loss coefficients for the fittings. In turbulent flow the friction factor depends on both Reynolds number and relative roughness through the implicit Colebrook correlation — or, more practically, the Moody chart. Remember roughness is irrelevant in laminar flow.

Summing these losses is what builds the system curve: a static lift plus a term growing roughly as $Q^2$.

Comes back in Week 2, where the system curve crossing the pump curve gives the operating point; and Week 5, which re-derives the wall shear underlying $f$. ENGG2300 Week 7 →

8 · Vapour pressure and cavitation

A liquid boils when its local pressure falls to its vapour pressure — and $p_v$ climbs steeply with temperature, so hot liquids cavitate far more readily. Bubbles formed in the low-pressure region collapse violently downstream, eroding the surface.

You need two things from ENGG2300 here: that hydrostatic and friction terms determine the pressure at a pump inlet, and that the relevant comparison is in absolute pressure.

Comes back in Week 1, immediately — NPSH is exactly this calculation, and positioning a pump so the available NPSH exceeds the required value is a core design task. ENGG2300 Week 1 →

9 · No-slip, boundary layers and separation

Every real fluid satisfies the no-slip condition at a wall, which creates the boundary layer — the thin region where viscosity has slowed the flow. It grows along a surface, starts laminar and may go turbulent. Outside it, the flow behaves as if inviscid.

Two results to carry forward: a turbulent boundary layer resists separation better because it carries more momentum near the wall; and where the layer separates, a wide low-pressure wake forms and pressure drag dominates.

Comes back in Week 5, which takes this much further — the law of the wall, friction velocity, momentum and displacement thickness. ENGG2300 Week 6 →

10 · The Navier–Stokes equations

$$\nabla\!\cdot\!\vec V = 0 \qquad \rho\!\left(\frac{\partial\vec V}{\partial t} + \vec V\!\cdot\!\nabla\vec V\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\vec V + \rho\vec g$$

You do not need to solve these by hand in MECH3780, but you do need to recognise the terms and know two facts about them: there is no general analytical solution, and the nonlinear convective term $\vec V\!\cdot\!\nabla\vec V$ is why.

Comes back in Week 5, where time-averaging these equations produces the Reynolds stresses and hence the closure problem; and Week 7, where a solver discretises them over a mesh. ENGG2300 Week 5 →

11 · Dimensional analysis and similarity

The Buckingham Pi theorem reduces $k$ variables in $r$ dimensions to $k-r$ dimensionless groups, and dynamic similarity says two flows behave alike when the governing group matches.

Comes back in Week 1 — and this connection is worth making explicitly, because it is easy to miss. The pump similarity (affinity) rules, scaling flow with $nD^3$, head with $n^2D^2$ and power with $\rho n^3D^5$, are not arbitrary empirical laws — they are the output of exactly this dimensional analysis applied to a family of geometrically similar machines.
