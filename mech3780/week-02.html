Turbomachinery 2 — Systems & Turbines

A pump curve on its own tells you nothing about what the pump will actually do — that depends on the circuit it is fitted to. This week matches pumps to systems, combines them, and then reverses the machine to extract energy instead of adding it.

Matching a pump to a system

Apply the energy equation between the two reservoir surfaces: the pump must supply the static lift plus every loss along the way.

$$H_{sys}(Q) = \Delta z + \left(f\frac{L}{D} + \sum K\right)\frac{V^2}{2g}$$

Since $V \propto Q$, the loss term grows roughly as $Q^2$ — the system curve is a rising parabola sitting on the static lift. The pump settles where both conditions hold simultaneously:

$$Q_{pump} = Q_{sys} \qquad H_{pump} = H_{sys}$$

which is the intersection of the two curves: the operating (duty) point. Reading across from it gives the flow, head, efficiency and power draw all at once.

Design check A pump is well chosen when its duty point sits near the BEP. Far to either side and you lose efficiency, and at very low flow you risk recirculation and cavitation.

Controlling a pump

Two ways to move the duty point:

  • Change the system curve — throttle a valve on the discharge. This steepens the parabola and slides the duty point back along the pump curve. Simple, but the throttling loss is wasted energy.
  • Change the pump curve — vary the shaft speed $n$. The similarity rules shift the whole H–Q curve down and left, meeting the unchanged system curve at a lower duty. Since power scales as $n^3$, this is far more efficient — the reason variable-speed drives are standard.

Pumps in series and parallel

$$\text{Series: } Q = Q_1 = Q_2,\ \ H = H_1 + H_2 \qquad\qquad \text{Parallel: } H = H_1 = H_2,\ \ Q = Q_1 + Q_2$$

Series machines pass the same flow and add their heads — use them when the system needs more lift. Parallel machines share the head and add their flows — use them when it needs more throughput (and they give redundancy).

The catch Two pumps in parallel do not give twice the flow. Adding flow pushes the operating point up the steepening system curve, so each machine delivers less than it would alone: $Q_1 + Q_2 < Q_I + Q_{II}$. The same logic limits series pumping. Always find the new intersection rather than doubling.

Turbines

A turbine extracts energy from high-head fluid — but it is not simply a pump run backwards. Two families, distinguished by how the head is converted:

  • Reaction turbines — the blade passages run full and the pressure drop happens inside the runner. Radial, mixed and axial designs: the Francis (mixed flow, medium head) and the Kaplan (axial, propeller-like, low head and high flow). These are dynamic machines that admit high-energy fluid and take its momentum.
  • Impulse turbines — a nozzle first converts the head into a high-velocity jet, which strikes the buckets one at a time at essentially atmospheric pressure. Suits very high head with low flow (the Pelton wheel).

Force of a jet on a blade

The impulse machine is analysed with the momentum theorem on a control volume moving with the bucket. For a jet of speed $V_j$ striking a blade moving at $u$ and turned through angle $\theta$:

$$F = \rho Q\,(V_j - u)(1 - \cos\theta) \qquad P = F u$$

Two results fall out: deflection is most effective as $\theta \to 180^\circ$ (hence the split-bucket shape of a Pelton wheel), and differentiating the power shows it peaks when the bucket runs at about half the jet speed.

Selecting a turbine The tutorial asks you to pick type, number and size for a given net head and flow with a fixed generator speed — that is really a specific-speed argument: high head and low flow favours impulse, low head and high flow favours Kaplan, with Francis between.
