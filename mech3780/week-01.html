Turbomachinery 1 — Pumps

The aim is elementary engineering estimates of fluid-machine performance, for nearly incompressible flow. We care about the flow principles, not the detailed mechanical construction — enough to size a pump, read its curve, and keep it from destroying itself.

Classifying pumps

Two families, with very different characters:

  • Positive-displacement pumps (PDPs) — a cavity opens, traps and squeezes the fluid, so flow is pulsating but near-constant. Reciprocating (piston, plunger, diaphragm) or rotary (gear, lobe, screw, sliding vane, peristaltic). They reach very high pressures (~300 atm) at low flow (~100 gal/min), and handle any viscosity.
  • Dynamic pumps — fast-moving blades add momentum, which a diffuser converts to pressure. Radial (centrifugal), axial or mixed flow. Very high flow (up to 300,000 gal/min) at moderate pressure rise, but high viscosity degrades them sharply.
Reading the p–Q comparison A PDP gives essentially constant flow with virtually unlimited pressure rise — which is why PDPs dominate metering. A dynamic pump varies continuously from maximum head at shutoff to zero head at maximum flow.

The centrifugal pump

Fluid enters axially through the eye, is caught by the impeller blades and whirled tangentially and radially outward, gaining both velocity and pressure. It leaves into the doughnut-shaped scroll (volute), which decelerates the flow and converts more of that velocity into pressure.

Output parameters

The net head is the Bernoulli-head rise across the machine, and multiplying by $\rho g Q$ gives the power actually delivered to the fluid:

$$H = \left(\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z\right)_2 - \left(\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z\right)_1 \qquad P_w = \rho g Q H$$

If suction and discharge pipes match in size and height, the velocity and elevation terms cancel. Real shaft power (brake horsepower) exceeds $P_w$, and the shortfall splits three ways:

$$\eta = \frac{P_w}{bhp} = \eta_v\,\eta_h\,\eta_m$$

volumetric (leakage), hydraulic (friction and shock in the flow) and mechanical (bearings and seals).

Elementary pump theory

Assume one-dimensional flow and apply the angular-momentum theorem to a control volume around the impeller. Fluid enters at $r_1$ with relative velocity $w_1$ tangent to the blade plus tip speed $u_1 = \omega r_1$; their vector sum is the absolute velocity $V_1$. Same at exit. That gives the Euler relations:

$$T = \rho Q(r_2V_{t2} - r_1V_{t1}) \qquad P = \omega T \qquad H = \frac{u_2V_{t2} - u_1V_{t1}}{g}$$

with continuity through the impeller $Q = 2\pi r_1 b_1 V_{n1} = 2\pi r_2 b_2 V_{n2}$ and, from the exit triangle, $V_{t2} = u_2 - V_{n2}\cot\beta_2$. At the design point flow enters radially, so $\alpha_1 = 90^\circ$ and $V_{t1}=0$ — which kills the second term and simplifies everything.

Draw the triangles The lecture is explicit about this: sketch both velocity triangles with every component labelled — $\alpha$, $\beta$, $u$, $w$, $V$, $V_t$. Nearly every marking scheme rewards this.

Blade angle and performance curves

Substituting continuity into the Euler head gives a straight line in $Q$:

$$H = \frac{u_2^2}{g} - \frac{u_2\cot\beta_2}{2\pi r_2 b_2\, g}\,Q$$

with a shutoff head of $u_2^2/g$, sloping down for backward-curved blades ($\beta_2 < 90^\circ$) and up for forward-curved. Real measured curves add the losses: efficiency is zero at both no-flow and $Q_{max}$, peaking 80–90% at roughly $0.6\,Q_{max}$ — the best efficiency point. Backward-curved blades level off in power above the BEP (a "non-overloading" curve), which is why they are preferred.

Cavitation and NPSH

Cavitation is vapour bubbles forming where the pressure drops below the liquid's vapour pressure; carried into higher pressure they collapse and erode the blades. The guard against it is net positive-suction head:

$$NPSH = \frac{p_i}{\rho g} + \frac{V_i^2}{2g} - \frac{p_v}{\rho g} = \frac{p_a}{\rho g} - z_i - h_f - \frac{p_v}{\rho g}$$

The right-hand form comes from Bernoulli back to the free surface and is what the installation provides — so position the pump (reduce $z_i$ and the suction friction $h_f$) until the available NPSH comfortably exceeds the manufacturer's required value. Note that $p_v$ climbs steeply with temperature, so hot liquids cavitate far more readily.

Similarity rules

Dimensional analysis says head and power depend on $Q$, impeller diameter $D$, speed $n$, density, viscosity and roughness. For geometrically similar pumps that collapses to:

$$\frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}\!\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^{\!3} \qquad \frac{H_2}{H_1} = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)^{\!2}\!\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^{\!2} \qquad \frac{P_2}{P_1} = \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\!\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)^{\!3}\!\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^{\!5}$$

so one tested curve rescales across a whole pump family. Note the strong dependences: power goes as speed cubed and diameter to the fifth.

Units The lecture examples are worked in US units (inches, gal/min, horsepower) and then explicitly ask you to convert to SI. 1 US gallon ≈ 3.785 L.
