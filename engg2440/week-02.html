Model Representations & the Generalised Framework

This week contains the single most important idea in the modelling half: every physical domain runs on the same two variables. Once you see force/velocity, torque/angular-velocity, voltage/current and pressure/flow as the same pair wearing different clothes, each new domain costs you almost nothing.

Three representations

The same linear system can be written as a block diagram, an ODE, or a state-space model, and you must be able to move between them. Converting an $n$th-order ODE to state-space is mechanical: take the output and its first $n-1$ derivatives as states. The order of a model is the number of states — equivalently the order of the governing ODE.

Power ports and power variables

Components interact by exchanging energy. At each point of interaction — a power port — two variables multiply to give power:

$$P_W(t) = e(t)\,f(t)$$

We call $e$ the generalised effort and $f$ the generalised flow. Some components have one port; a DC motor has two (an electrical port carrying $V\!I$ and a mechanical one carrying $T\omega$).

Energy variables — why those states?

Integrating the power variables gives the energy variables: generalised momentum (the integral of effort) and generalised displacement (the integral of flow).

$$p(t) = \int e(t)\,dt \qquad q(t) = \int f(t)\,dt$$

This finally answers why we chose momentum and position as states last week. In every domain we select the energy variables as the states — so state derivatives are power variables, and solving the model means watching the system's energy evolve.

Rotational mechanical systems

The rotational domain is the translational one with new labels: angular momentum $L$ and angular displacement $\theta$ are the energy variables, and the CCRs mirror exactly:

$$L = J\omega,\quad \dot L = T \qquad T = k\theta \qquad T = b\omega \qquad J = \int_Q r^2\,dm$$

Unlike point masses, shape matters — captured by the moment of inertia $J$, the rotational analogue of mass. Two masses on the same shaft share a speed and are not disconnected, so they count as one degree of freedom.

The generalised elements

Every component you will meet is one of six archetypes:

  • Resistors $e = R(f)$ — dissipate energy (damper, resistor, valve). Often linear, but not always: viscous friction $F = bv|v|$ is not.
  • Inertias $e = \dot{\mathcal I}(f)$ — store energy via momentum (mass, inertia, inductor, fluid in a pipe).
  • Capacitors $e = \mathcal C(q)$ — store energy via displacement (spring, capacitor, tank).
  • Sources — supply one power variable regardless of the other. An idealisation implying infinite power, so always ask whether your system meaningfully loads the source.
  • Transformers — map effort→effort and flow→flow (lever, gearbox, piston). Ideal if $e_1f_1 = e_2f_2$.
  • Gyrators — swap the roles, mapping effort→flow (the DC motor of Week 3).
