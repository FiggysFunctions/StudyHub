Frequency Response & Bode Plots

A different lens on the same systems. Drive a stable linear system with a sine and, once the transient dies, you get a sine back — same frequency, different amplitude and phase. Sweeping the frequency traces out the frequency response, from which stability and robustness can be read directly.

Sine in, sine out

Any response splits into transient and steady state, $y(t) = y_{tr}(t) + y_{ss}(t)$, and for a stable system the transient decays. For a sinusoidal input the steady-state part is a sinusoid at the same frequency:

$$u(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_{ss}(t) = |G(j\omega)|\,A\,\sin\!\big(\omega t + \phi + \angle G(j\omega)\big)$$

Both the magnitude and phase depend on the excitation frequency. Negative phase means the output lags the input; positive means it leads. The frequency response function comes from substituting $s = j\omega$:

$$G(j\omega) = G(s)\big|_{s=j\omega} = G_R(\omega) + jG_I(\omega) = |G(j\omega)|\,e^{\,j\angle G(j\omega)}$$
Why this is practical Unlike pole locations, the frequency response can be measured on real hardware — drive the system at a series of frequencies and record amplitude ratio and phase shift. No model required.

Bode plots

A Bode plot is two graphs against a logarithmic frequency axis: magnitude in decibels, and phase in degrees.

$$|G(j\omega)|_{dB} = 20\log_{10}|G(j\omega)|$$

The logarithm is the whole trick. Cascaded systems multiply their magnitudes and add their phases — but in decibels the magnitudes add too. So a transfer function can be broken into its elementary factors (gains, integrators, first-order poles and zeros, second-order pairs), each sketched as straight-line asymptotes, and the pieces simply summed.

Useful asymptotic rules: a constant gain is a horizontal line at $20\log_{10}K$; an integrator $1/s$ is $-20$ dB/decade with $-90^\circ$ phase; a simple pole breaks downward by $-20$ dB/decade at its corner frequency, contributing $-90^\circ$ spread over roughly two decades; a zero does the mirror image upward.

Stability from the open loop

The closed-loop transfer function usually is not known until the controller is finalised — but the open-loop response is available. The closed-loop characteristic equation is $1 + G(s)C(s) = 0$, i.e.

$$G(s)C(s) = -1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad |C(j\omega)G(j\omega)| = 1 \ \text{ and }\ \angle C(j\omega)G(j\omega) = \pm180^\circ$$

So the critical point is $-1$, and how close the open-loop response passes to it measures how close the closed loop is to instability.

Gain and phase margin

  • Gain margin — find the phase-crossover frequency where the phase is $-180^\circ$; the gain margin is how far the magnitude curve sits below 0 dB there, i.e. the extra gain that would destabilise the loop.
  • Phase margin — find the gain-crossover frequency where the magnitude is 0 dB; the phase margin is how far the phase there is above $-180^\circ$:
$$\phi_M = 180^\circ + \angle G(j\omega_{pm})C(j\omega_{pm})$$

Larger margins mean the loop tolerates more change in the plant before going unstable — the frequency-domain expression of robustness, and qualitatively the same message as poles being far from the imaginary axis.
