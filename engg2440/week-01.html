Modelling & State-Space Models

Engineers learn about the world by building mathematical models — cheaper and faster than repeated real-world testing, and sometimes the only option at all. This week sets up what a model is, introduces the state-space form that everything in the modelling half targets, and gives the structured procedure that makes even complicated systems routine.

What a model is — and validating it

A mathematical model is a set of rules relating system variables. Inputs drive change (some controllable like an applied voltage, some not — gravity, road bumps — which we call disturbances); outputs are the variables we want to track. Models can be theoretical (from physics), empirical (black box, inferred from data), or a mix.

"All models are wrong, but some are useful" A model is only as good as its assumptions. Validation against experimental data is what earns you the right to make decisions from it — an unvalidated model should not be used.

The state-space model

The state of a system is a set of variables that, at an instant, are sufficient to determine every other dependent variable. We collect them into a state vector $\mathbf z(t)$, and the target of every model we build is:

$$\dot{\mathbf z}(t) = f(\mathbf z(t),\mathbf u(t),t) \qquad \mathbf y(t) = g(\mathbf z(t),\mathbf u(t),t)$$

These are coupled first-order ODEs. The form is easy to simulate numerically — integrate from an initial state — and a solution carries a great deal of information, since from $\mathbf z(t)$ any other variable follows.

The bullet-proof modelling procedure

First-principles modelling can feel like hoarding equations until something works. The course replaces that with a fixed seven-step recipe, applied identically in every physical domain:

  1. Inputs — list everything that drives the system. To call something an input you must know its value at every instant.
  2. Outputs — list what you want to know. These can be added later without re-modelling.
  3. States — choose the variables the model will be written around (Week 2 gives the general rule).
  4. CCRs — the governing equation of each component in isolation.
  5. SSRs — how the components are interconnected.
  6. State equations — differentiate each state and substitute CCRs and SSRs until it depends only on states, inputs, time and constants.
  7. Output equations — do the same for each output.
Where the marks are CCRs are largely equations other people derived. The SSRs are where you do the physics — and that is where most of the marks in a quiz sit.

Translational mechanical systems

We start in the simplest domain: bodies free only to translate, treated as point masses in one dimension. State counting follows the degrees of freedom — the independent ways the disconnected masses can move:

$$N_{DOF} = n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_N \qquad N_{states} = 2 \times N_{DOF}$$

Masses rigidly linked are not disconnected and count once. The three basic components give us our first CCRs:

$$\underbrace{P = mv,\quad \dot P = F}_{\text{mass (inertia)}} \qquad \underbrace{F = k\Delta}_{\text{spring}} \qquad \underbrace{F = bv}_{\text{damper}}$$

The mass stores kinetic energy $E_k=\tfrac12 mv^2$, the spring stores potential energy $E_p=\tfrac12 k\Delta^2$, and the damper dissipates. The SSR for a mass is Newton's second law, applied by drawing a free-body diagram.

Sign discipline Momentum, velocity and net force must all be taken positive in the same direction. Draw the direction of motion on your free-body diagram — even if the algebra goes wrong afterwards, that earns partial marks.
Where this leads Week 2 reveals why momentum and displacement are the natural states, and generalises every component into a handful of archetypes that work in any domain.
