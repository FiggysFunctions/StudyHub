Fluid Power Systems

The last physical domain: systems that use a nearly incompressible fluid — water or hydraulic oil — to transmit power. Hydraulics dominate where large forces must move quickly; a hydraulic motor can produce roughly ten times the torque of an electric motor of similar size.

Slotting fluid power into the framework

$$\text{Effort} \to P(t) \qquad \text{Flow} \to Q(t) \qquad \text{Momentum} \to P_p(t) \qquad \text{Displacement} \to V(t)$$

Pressure is the effort and volumetric flow rate the flow, so their product is power. The energy variables are pressure momentum (the momentum of fluid moving along a pipe) and stored volume. As in the electrical domain, the number of states equals the number of independent energy-storing elements — the tanks and the long pipes.

Fluid inertia

These systems are usually high-pressure and low-flow, so inertia is often ignored — but in a long, thin pipe the mass of the fluid matters. Treating that fluid column as a mass driven by the pressure difference across it, $\rho A l \dot v = A\,\Delta P$, and writing velocity in terms of flow rate $Q = vA$:

$$\mathcal I\,\dot Q(t) = \Delta P(t), \qquad \mathcal I = \frac{\rho\,l}{A}$$

which is only appreciable for small area and large length — exactly the intuition that a long narrow pipe is hard to start and stop flow in.

Capacitance, resistance and compressibility

A tank or accumulator is the generalised capacitor: volume is the state, its derivative is the flow rate, and pressure rises as it fills.

$$\dot V = Q \qquad P = \frac{V}{C}$$

Valves and orifices are the generalised resistors, relating pressure drop to flow — often non-linearly, which is one of the main sources of nonlinearity the course later linearises. Where fluid compressibility matters it is modelled by hanging a dummy accumulator off the pressure side of a chamber.

Piston-cylinders as transformers

Cylinders are the transformers connecting fluid power to the mechanical domains — pressure becomes force, velocity becomes flow, both scaled by the plunger area. Neglecting the plunger mass, a free-body diagram gives:

$$\text{Single-acting: } F = P A_p,\quad Q = v A_p \qquad\qquad \text{Symmetric: } F = (P_1 - P_2)A_p$$

An asymmetric cylinder differs only in the exposed face area on each side — the rod occupies part of one face — so a higher pressure is needed on the plunger side to produce the same force. Check the drawn positive directions: as usually drawn, positive velocity pushes fluid out of the cylinder (positive $Q$).

Domain complete Mechanical (translational and rotational), electrical and fluid power — with transformers and gyrators to join them, you can now model multi-domain systems such as a valve-controlled hydraulic actuator driven by a motorised pump.
Where this leads Week 5 linearises these (often nonlinear) models and converts them to transfer functions, which is the bridge into the control half.
