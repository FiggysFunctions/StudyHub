Thin-Walled Pressure Vessels

Gas bottles, boilers, pipes, submarines, aircraft fuselages, balloons: pressurised shells are everywhere, and when the wall is thin the stress analysis becomes beautifully simple. A cut-and-balance argument gives the membrane stresses directly, and everything from Weeks 7 and 9 — Mohr's circle, out-of-plane shear, von Mises design — plugs straight in.

The thin-wall assumptions

A thin-walled pressure vessel is one where the wall thickness is insignificant compared with the radius, $t \ll R$ (roughly $R/t > 10$). Three consequences follow:

First, loads are carried purely by tangential tension or compression in the wall — a membrane action, meaning the wall is in plane stress. Second, there is no variation of stress through the thickness: the wall is too thin for a gradient to matter. Third, the wall cannot support bending loads — like a balloon skin, it only works in tension.

Two simplified geometries cover most practice: cylindrical (storage tanks, pipes, gas bottles) and spherical (high-pressure gas spheres). Note that all pressures in these formulas are gauge pressures — the atmosphere pushes on the outside of the vessel too, so only the excess loads the wall.

Spherical vessels

Cut a pressurised sphere of radius $R$ and wall thickness $t$ in half and balance forces along the cut axis. The pressure acts on the projected disc area $\pi R^2$; the wall resists over its thin annulus $2\pi R t$:

$$\sum F_z = 0 = 2\pi R t\,\sigma_h - \pi R^2 P \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_h = \frac{PR}{2t}$$

By symmetry the same answer comes out whichever way you slice, so the membrane stress is $PR/2t$ in every in-plane direction. Every direction is principal, and the in-plane Mohr's circle collapses to a single point at $(\sigma_h, 0)$. See spherical vessel stress.

Don't forget the third dimension. On the inner surface the pressure pushes directly on the wall, so the through-thickness principal stress is $-P$ (zero at the outer surface). The full 3D Mohr picture is one circle from $-P$ to $\sigma_h$, giving a maximum shear stress that is entirely out of plane:

$$\tau_{\max} = \tfrac12(\sigma_h + P) = \frac{PR}{4t}\Big(1 + 2\tfrac{t}{R}\Big) \approx \frac{PR}{4t} \qquad (t \ll R)$$

Cylindrical vessels — hoop stress

For a long cylinder ($L \gg R$), slice it lengthwise and consider half the shell. Over a length $L$, the pressure pushes on the projected rectangle $2RL$ and is resisted by the wall on the two longitudinal cuts, each of area $Lt$:

$$\sum F_x = 0 = 2Lt\,\sigma_h - 2RLP \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_h = \frac{PR}{t}$$

This circumferential stress is the hoop stress — the largest stress in the vessel, and the reason overloaded cylinders split along their length like an overcooked sausage. See hoop stress on the equation sheet.

Cylindrical vessels — axial stress

Now cut the cylinder transversely. The pressure on the end cap, $\pi R^2 P$, is carried by the wall annulus $2\pi R t$ — the same balance as the sphere:

$$\sum F_z = 0 = 2\pi R t\,\sigma_a - \pi R^2 P \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_a = \frac{PR}{2t} = \frac{\sigma_h}{2}$$

The axial (longitudinal) stress is exactly half the hoop stress, so hoop always governs a cylinder's thickness. Because $\sigma_h$ and $\sigma_a$ act on planes with no shear between them, they are the in-plane principal stresses of the wall element. See axial stress.

Watch out The axial stress exists only if the pressure actually loads the ends of the cylinder. In Hibbeler 8-3 the same cylinder is supported two ways: (a) the piston load passes through the fluid straight to the base, so the wall carries hoop stress only ($\sigma_a = 0$); (b) the wall hangs from an end cap, so it carries both $\sigma_h = PR/t$ and $\sigma_a = PR/2t$. Always sketch the free body before writing $PR/2t$.

Mohr's circles and maximum shear

The cylindrical wall element has principal stresses $\sigma_h$, $\sigma_a = \sigma_h/2$ and, through the thickness, $-P \approx 0$. Its three-dimensional Mohr's circles are therefore nested: a small in-plane circle between $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_h$, and the governing large circle from $-P$ to $\sigma_h$. The true maximum shear is out of plane, at $45^\circ$ through the thickness:

$$\tau_{\max} = \tfrac12(\sigma_h + P) = \frac{PR}{2t}\Big(1 + \tfrac{t}{R}\Big) \approx \frac{PR}{2t} \qquad (t \ll R)$$

This is the same out-of-plane trap flagged in Week 7: quoting the in-plane radius $(\sigma_h - \sigma_a)/2 = PR/4t$ understates the real maximum by a factor of two. For the sphere, the in-plane circle is a point, so all the shear is out-of-plane and $\tau_{\max} \approx PR/4t$. Both results live at maximum shear in vessel walls.

Welded seams and helical joints

Vessels are fabricated from rolled plate joined by welds, and the weld is usually the weak point: designs limit the normal (and sometimes shear) stress on the weld plane. Since the wall's principal stresses are known ($\sigma_h$, $\sigma_a$), the stress on a seam at any angle comes straight from Week 7 stress transformation or a spin around Mohr's circle.

Beer 7.114/7.115 (a helical weld at $\beta = 30^\circ$) and 7.116 (plates joined either in a square grid or on $45^\circ$ diagonals) are exactly this: express $\sigma_h = 37.5P$ and $\sigma_a = 18.75P$ in terms of the unknown pressure, rotate to the weld plane, set the result equal to the allowable, and solve for the largest gauge pressure $P$. Adding a torque to the vessel (7.120, 7.124) simply superposes a $Tr/J$ shear on the element before transforming — combined loading from Week 6 meeting pressure vessels.

Bands, shrink fits and thermal stress

A band or ring fitted snugly around a rigid cylinder becomes a pressure-vessel problem in reverse: force the band to a strain and ask what hoop stress — and what contact pressure — results.

In Hibbeler 8-9, a stainless band on a rigid cylinder is cooled by a nonuniform temperature drop $\Delta T = 12\sin^2\theta$ °C. The free thermal contraction of the circumference is found by integrating the thermal strain around the loop, $\Delta L = \int_0^{2\pi} \alpha\,\Delta T \, R\,d\theta$. The rigid cylinder prevents that contraction, so the band carries an average tensile hoop strain $\varepsilon = \Delta L / 2\pi R$ and hence a hoop stress $\sigma_h = E\varepsilon \approx 19.2$ MPa. Running the hoop equilibrium backwards, $P = \sigma_h t / R$, gives the contact pressure the band squeezes onto the cylinder (about 30.7 kPa).

Beer 7.126 is the two-material version — a brass ring inside a steel ring, heated together. Each ring expands differently, compatibility forces them to the same radius, and the interface pressure follows. The logic is identical to the statically indeterminate thermal problems of Week 1, wrapped into a circle.

Design example: the LPG tank

The lecture's capstone problem designs a 1.5 m-diameter, 15 m-span LPG tank (1 MPa gauge, liquid density 550 kg/m³, steel with $\sigma_y = 300$ MPa, safety factor 3). It stitches together half the subject:

Gravity load. The weight per metre of steel shell plus liquid, $w = \rho_{\text{gas}} g A + \rho_{\text{steel}} g\,(2\pi R t)$, makes the tank a simply supported beam. The SFD/BMD give a midspan moment $M = \tfrac{7.5^2}{2}w$, and the bending stress follows from the flexure formula with the thin annulus $I = \tfrac{\pi}{64}(d_o^4 - d_i^4)$.

Pressure load. Hoop $\sigma_h = 0.75\times10^6/t$ and axial $\sigma_a = 0.375\times10^6/t$, with the gravity bending stress adding to the axial direction at the extreme fibre.

Failure check. The wall is in plane stress with principal stresses $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_h$, so the Week 9 von Mises equivalent stress is

$$\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_h^2 - \sigma_a \sigma_h} \;\le\; \frac{\sigma_y}{3}$$

Plotting axial, hoop and von Mises stress against thickness and intersecting with the allowable $\sigma_y/3 = 100$ MPa gives $t_{\min} = 6.76$ mm. A real design would round up to the next plate size and check the supports, welds and corrosion allowance — but the mechanics is all here.

Where this leads Pressure vessels are the cleanest real-world showcase of the whole toolkit: equilibrium cuts give the stresses, Mohr's circle finds the true maximum shear (mind the out-of-plane trap), stress transformation handles welds, and von Mises with a safety factor sizes the wall. Quiz 3 (Week 12) covers Weeks 9–11, and hoop/axial stress questions are near-certain to appear.
