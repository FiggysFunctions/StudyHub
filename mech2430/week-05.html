Transverse Loading & Shear in Beams

Real beams rarely see pure bending: a transverse load produces a shear force $V$ alongside the bending moment $M$. This week is about the shear stresses that $V$ creates — why they must also act along the beam, how to compute them with $VQ/I$, how they size the nails and glue in built-up beams, and what happens to shear once a section starts to yield.

Transverse loading: shear plus bending

Cut a beam carrying a transverse load $P$ at a distance $x$ from the load and equilibrium of the free body demands both an internal moment and an internal shear force:

$$M = Px \qquad V = P$$

The sign convention: $V$ is positive when it acts downward on the left-hand side of the section. Summing the stresses over the cross-section, the vertical shear stresses must balance the shear force, $\int_A \tau_{xy}\,dA = -V$, while the horizontal ones sum to zero, $\int_A \tau_{xz}\,dA = 0$ — but note that an average of zero does not mean $\tau_{xz}=0$ everywhere. We will meet significant $\tau_{xz}$ in flanges later.

Longitudinal shear: the plank picture

Because the stress tensor is symmetric, a vertical shear stress $\tau_{xy}$ at a point must be accompanied by an equal shear stress $\tau_{yx}$ on the horizontal plane through the same point. Transverse loading therefore produces longitudinal shear stresses.

The classic demonstration: bend a stack of loose planks and they slide over one another — each plank bends independently. Clamp them together and the sliding is prevented; the clamping surfaces are now carrying longitudinal shear. Nails, bolts and glue in a built-up beam are doing exactly this clamping job, which is why we need to know how much shear they must resist.

Shear force on a horizontal plane

To quantify that longitudinal shear, cut a horizontal plane through a beam at height $y_1$ and consider the equilibrium of the material above it (area $A'$). The bending stresses $\sigma_x = Mxy/I$ on the end faces don't balance unless a longitudinal shear force $H$ acts on the cut plane:

$$\sum F_x:\quad 0 = H - \int_{A'} \sigma_x\,dA \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; H = \frac{VQ}{I}\,x$$

where the integral of $y$ over the cut-off area is the first moment of area:

$$Q = \int_{A'} y\,dA = A'\bar{y}$$

$Q$ is the workhorse of this whole week: the area beyond the cut times the distance from its centroid to the neutral axis. It is zero at the top and bottom faces (no area beyond the cut) and largest at the neutral axis. Full form on the sheet: shear on a horizontal plane. Crucially, nothing in the derivation requires the cut to be horizontal — the same equilibrium argument works on any longitudinal surface, planar or not, which we exploit below.

Shear flow and built-up beams

Dividing $H$ by the length $x$ gives the longitudinal shear force per unit length of beam — the shear flow:

$$q = \frac{H}{x} = \frac{VQ}{I}$$

Shear flow is what sizes the joints of a built-up member. Discrete fasteners spaced $s$ apart must each carry the flow accumulated over one spacing, shared between however many parallel rows $n$ cross the joint:

$$F = \frac{q\,s}{n} = \frac{VQ}{I}\cdot\frac{s}{n}$$

See fastener force. When taking $Q$, use the first moment of the piece being held on by the joint.

Worked example Two 150 × 50 mm boards are nailed top and bottom with two rows of nails at 150 mm spacing, carrying $V = 3$ kN. With $I = \tfrac{1}{12}(150)(100^3) = 12.5\times10^6$ mm⁴ and $Q = 150\times50\times25 = 187.5\times10^3$ mm³, the shear flow is $q = VQ/I = 45$ kN/m. In one metre there are $2\times(1/0.15) = 13.33$ nails, so each nail resists $45/13.33 = 3.375$ kN — identical to $F = qs/n$ with $s=0.15$ m, $n=2$.

The tutorial problems run the same logic in every direction: given an allowable nail force, find the allowable $V$ (Beer 6.1, 6.3, 6.5) or the largest permissible spacing $s$ (6.29); given bolt diameter and allowable stress, convert allowable stress → allowable force → allowable shear (6.5, 6.7, 6.45, 6.46).

Shear stress: $VQ/It$

Spread the shear flow over the width $t$ of the cut and you get the average longitudinal shear stress — which, by symmetry of the stress tensor, equals the transverse shear stress at the same point:

$$\tau_{xy} = \tau_{yx} = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta A} = \frac{VQ}{It}$$

Four notes worth memorising (see transverse shear stress):

① The shear stress on a horizontal plane equals the shear stress on the transverse plane. ② Shear stress is zero on the top and bottom faces — they are free surfaces. ③ The maximum does not necessarily occur on the neutral axis: $\tau$ depends on both $Q$ and $t$, and a narrow width can beat a large $Q$. ④ There is little variation across the width provided the section is narrow compared with its depth.

Watch out In glued box beams (tutorial 6.30) and bolted assemblies, $t$ is the total width of material actually cut — e.g. two 20 mm walls give $t = 40$ mm. Forgetting to double the thickness at a two-sided joint halves your answer.

Rectangular sections

For a $b \times h$ rectangle (half-height $Y$), $Q = \tfrac12 b(Y^2 - y^2)$ and $I = \tfrac23 bY^3$, so the distribution is parabolic:

$$\tau_{xy} = \frac{3}{2}\frac{V}{A}\left(1 - \frac{y^2}{Y^2}\right) \qquad \tau_{\max} = \frac{3}{2}\frac{V}{A}$$

Zero at top and bottom, peaking on the neutral axis at 1.5 times the average $V/A$. If $b \le \tfrac14 h$ the stress varies less than 20% from the average across the width, so the formula is a good pointwise estimate for slender sections. See rectangular-section shear.

Sections where the peak leaves the neutral axis

For non-rectangular shapes, evaluate $\tau = VQ/It$ as a function of position and look for the true maximum. A triangular section (base 40 mm, height 50 mm, $I = \tfrac{1}{36}bh^3$) under 100 kN is the lecture example: both $Q$ and $t$ vary with the distance $x$ from the apex, and the maximum of 150 MPa lands at $x = 25$ mm — halfway down, not on the neutral axis (which sits at 33.3 mm from the apex). This is note ③ above in action.

Wide-flange I-beams

Run the same process on an I-section and the shear stress is small and roughly uniform in the flanges but large and nearly constant down the web. Two design consequences: it is usually assumed that the web carries the entire shear load (so $\tau_{\text{ave}} \approx V/A_{\text{web}}$), and yet the horizontal component of shear in the flange still has a significant value — which the vertical-plane analysis below picks up.

Shear on arbitrary and vertical planes

Because the horizontal-plane derivation only used equilibrium of the material beyond a longitudinal cut, the identical result holds for a cut on any longitudinal surface:

$$q = \frac{H}{x} = \frac{VQ}{I} \qquad\qquad \tau_{xz} = \tau_{zx} \approx \tau_{\text{ave}} = \frac{VQ}{It}$$

Cut a vertical longitudinal plane through the flange of an I-beam, take $Q$ of the flange material beyond it, and you get the horizontal shear stress $\tau_{xz}$ there. Non-planar cuts work too: in the glued four-board beam of the lecture example ($V = 850$ kN), the glue line at the top joint is found from a straight cut (using symmetry, each of B and B′ takes half the flow of the whole flange: $q_B = 1314$ kN/m), while the lower joint C needs a non-planar section wrapped around the small board ($q_C = 49.8$ kN/m).

Shear-flow distribution in thin-walled sections

Tracking $q = VQ/I$ around a thin-walled section reveals a consistent pattern, using the channel of the lecture example ($V = 20$ kN):

Starting where the section ends (a free edge) or on the axis of symmetry, $Q = 0$ so $q = 0$. Moving along a flange, $Q$ grows in proportion to the length swept, so $q$ increases linearly (in the channel: $q_x = 806.2x$ kN/m, reaching 160 kN/m at the corner). Turning down a web or leg, the swept area's centroid distance changes with height, so $q$ varies parabolically. Because $t$ is constant around such a section, the shear-stress picture is the same shape (peaking at 16 MPa in the example).

Integrating $\tau$ over each region gives the resultant force it carries — 14.5 kN along each half-flange and 9.1 kN down each leg in the example — and these resultants are what give rise to the shear-centre ideas met in later courses. They also confirm the vertical resultants sum back to the applied $V$.

Plastic deformation and the plastic hinge

As a transverse load grows, yield begins where $M$ is largest — the root of a cantilever — once $PL \ge M_y$, and the plastic zones spread toward the loaded end. For a rectangular section the elastoplastic relation from Week 3 applies at each position $x$:

$$Px = \tfrac{3}{2}M_y\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\frac{y_y^2}{Y^2}\right)$$

When the elastic core vanishes ($y_y = 0$) the section reaches the plastic hinge condition, $PL = M_p = \tfrac32 M_y$ — the largest load the beam can support based on normal stress (see elastoplastic bending).

Key insight In the plastic zones the normal stress is constant at $\sigma_y$, so the equilibrium argument gives $\Delta H = 0$: the plastic zone contains no shear stress. All the vertical shear must be supported by the shrinking elastic zone — shear stresses there rise faster than the load.

General loading and superposition

A real member may carry normal loads, torsion, bending and transverse shear all at once. Because every relation this term is linear (below yield), the stress state is found by the principle of superposition: compute the stresses from each load case separately — $P/A$, $Tr/J$, $My/I$, $VQ/It$ — and add the components acting on the same planes. Combining and interpreting those combined states (principal stresses, Mohr's circle) is precisely where Weeks 6–7 go next.

Where this leads $VQ/I$ and $VQ/It$ complete the stress toolbox: axial, torsional, bending and now transverse shear. Superposing them gives a full 2D stress state at a point, and transforming that state is the subject of the coming weeks. Quiz 2 (Week 8) covers Weeks 5–7 — the tutorial problems on nailed, bolted and glued built-up beams (Beer Ch. 6) are ideal practice.
