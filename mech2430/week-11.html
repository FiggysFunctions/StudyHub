Deflection of Beams

Week 3 told us how sharply a beam curves at any point — $1/\rho = M/EI$. This week we integrate that curvature along the beam to recover its whole deflected shape, the elastic curve $y(x)$. The same machinery then cracks a problem statics alone cannot: finding the reactions of statically indeterminate beams.

From curvature to the elastic curve

From the geometry of any plane curve, the radius of curvature at a point is

$$\rho = \frac{\left[1+\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^{2}\right]^{3/2}}{\left|\dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}\right|}$$

Beam deflections are small compared with the span, so the slope $dy/dx$ is tiny and $(dy/dx)^2 \approx 0$. The curvature then collapses to just the second derivative, and combining it with the Week-3 result $1/\rho = M/EI$ gives the governing equation of the elastic curve:

$$EI\,\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = M(x)$$

Here $y$ is the deflection of the neutral axis, $M(x)$ the internal bending moment from a section cut, and $EI$ the flexural rigidity. See curvature and the radius of curvature.

Double integration

Since $M(x)$ is known (draw the SFD and BMD first — they tell you the expression and where it changes), we simply integrate twice:

$$EI\,\frac{dy}{dx} = \int M(x)\,dx + C_1 \qquad\qquad EI\,y = \int\!\!\left(\int M(x)\,dx\right)dx + C_1 x + C_2$$

The first integration gives the slope $dy/dx$ — the beam's rotation in radians at each point — and the second gives the deflection. Each integration produces a constant, so a single-region beam carries two unknown constants, $C_1$ and $C_2$. Full statement on the sheet: elastic curve by double integration.

Boundary conditions

The constants come from boundary conditions — points where we already know the deflection or slope because of how the beam is supported:

$$\text{pin or roller: } y = 0 \qquad\qquad \text{fixed (built-in) end: } y = 0 \ \text{ and } \ \frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$

A cantilever therefore gets both of its conditions at the wall; a simply supported beam gets $y=0$ at each support. Count carefully: two constants need two conditions per region. Any conditions beyond what the constants require are not wasted — they are exactly what lets us solve statically indeterminate beams below.

The classic cantilever cases

Tutorial problems 9.1–9.4 (Beer) work through the standard cantilever loadings. For an end couple $M_0$ the moment is constant, $M(x)=M_0$, and both constants vanish at the wall, giving

$$y(x) = \frac{M_0 x^{2}}{2EI} \qquad y(L) = \frac{M_0 L^{2}}{2EI} \qquad \frac{dy}{dx}(L) = \frac{M_0 L}{EI}$$

For an end load $P$ (with $M(x) = P(x-L)$ measured from the wall) the same two integrations give the famous tip results, and a uniformly distributed load $w$ follows the same recipe:

$$y_{\text{tip}} = -\frac{PL^{3}}{3EI} \qquad\qquad y_{\text{tip}} = -\frac{wL^{4}}{8EI}$$

Problem 9.4's triangular load shows the general pattern: integrate the load twice to get $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ first, then twice more for slope and deflection. All the tip formulas are collected in cantilever standard results — worth committing to memory, since superposition solutions lean on them constantly.

Sanity check Slopes and deflections scale as $1/EI$: a stiffer section deflects less. If a computed deflection looks physically silly (metres for a steel beam), suspect a units slip in $I$ — see below.

Simply supported beams and symmetry

Hibbeler 12-3 is the benchmark: a central point load $P$ on a simply supported span $L$. On the left half $M(x)=Px/2$; integrating twice and applying $y=0$ at the pin plus — by symmetry — $dy/dx=0$ at mid-span gives

$$EIy = \frac{P x^{3}}{12} - \frac{P L^{2} x}{16} \qquad \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{A} = \frac{PL^{2}}{16EI} \qquad y_{\max} = -\frac{PL^{3}}{48EI}$$

The symmetry trick supplied a slope condition without ever leaving the first half of the beam. Move the load off-centre, though, and the shortcut dies — the maximum no longer sits under the load, and we need the two-region treatment next. See simply supported, central load.

Multiple regions and continuity conditions

Whenever the moment expression changes along the beam — at a point load, an applied couple, or an interior support — the integration must be done region by region, and every region brings its own pair of constants. A beam with two regions has four constants ($C_1\ldots C_4$), so it needs four conditions. The supports rarely supply that many; the rest come from continuity (matching) conditions at the join:

$$y_{\text{left}} = y_{\text{right}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\text{left}} = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\text{right}} \quad\text{at the boundary}$$

Physically: the beam cannot tear apart (deflection continuous) and cannot kink (slope continuous). Hibbeler 12-42 is the template — a 3 kN/m UDL over the 5 m span plus a 15 kN·m end couple on the 3 m overhang gives one cubic/quartic curve for $x\in(0,5)$ and a quadratic for $x\in(5,8)$, stitched together by matching $y$ and $dy/dx$ at $x=5$. Beer 9.13 and 9.14 run the same play with real numbers.

Watch out If, for algebraic convenience, the two regions use coordinates $x_1$ and $x_2$ measured towards each other from opposite ends (as in the asymmetric point-load example), the deflections still match directly but the slopes match with opposite signs: $dy/dx_1 = -\,dy/dx_2$ at the join. Forgetting the sign flip is the classic error in these problems.

Real numbers: units and W-shapes

Numerical problems (Beer 9.13, 9.14) quote a rolled-steel section — e.g. W360 × 44 or W100 × 19.3 — whose second moment of area $I$ is read from Appendix C. The table gives $I$ in $10^{6}\ \text{mm}^4$, and the deflection formula wants consistent SI units, so convert:

$$I = 4.77\times10^{6}\ \text{mm}^4 = 4.77\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4 \qquad (1\ \text{mm}^4 = 10^{-12}\ \text{m}^4)$$

With $E = 200$ GPa $= 200\times10^{9}$ Pa and moments written in N·m, the working of 9.14 lands at $y(0.8) \approx 0.051$ m $= 51$ mm at point $C$. A quick reality check against the span (3.2 m) tells you whether an answer is plausible — this small W100 section under a 38 kN·m couple really does sag that much, whereas the same answer from a W360 would scream "units error".

Statically indeterminate beams

Here is the payoff. Take a propped cantilever — fixed at one end, roller at the other, under a distributed load. The free body has three support unknowns ($M_A$, $R_A$, $R_B$) but planar equilibrium supplies only two equations: the beam is statically indeterminate to the first degree. Statics has run out — but the elastic curve has spare information. Counting everything:

$$\underbrace{M_A,\ R_A,\ R_B,\ C_1,\ C_2}_{5\ \text{unknowns}} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \underbrace{\textstyle\sum F_y = 0,\ \ \sum M = 0,\ \ y_A = 0,\ \ y_B = 0,\ \ \frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_A = 0}_{5\ \text{equations}}$$

Write $M(x)$ keeping the unknown reaction as a symbol, integrate twice, then apply all the boundary conditions: the surplus ones pin down the reaction itself. Sample Problem 9.3 (triangular load, pin at $A$, fixed at $B$) works through it: with $M(x) = R_A x - w_0 x^{3}/6L$, the conditions $y(0)=0$, $y(L)=0$ and $dy/dx(L)=0$ yield

$$R_A = \frac{w_0 L}{10} \qquad R_B = \frac{2 w_0 L}{5} \qquad M_B = \frac{w_0 L^{2}}{15}$$

after which equilibrium mops up the remaining reactions and superposition-style bookkeeping gives the full elastic curve. Tutorial problems 9.19–9.22 (reaction at the roller) and 9.33–9.34 (reaction plus BMD for fixed–fixed beams) all follow this recipe; the fixed–fixed cases simply trade the roller's single condition for a second slope condition.

Where this leads Deflection by integration completes the bending story: stresses from Week 3, shear from Week 5, and now stiffness and indeterminate reactions. Together with failure criteria (Week 9) and pressure vessels (Week 10) this is the material for Quiz 3 in the Week 12 tutorial — practise the boundary-condition bookkeeping until it is automatic.
