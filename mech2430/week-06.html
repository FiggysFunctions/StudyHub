Combined Loading & Superposition

Real components rarely feel just one kind of load. A crankshaft carries torque, bending and shear at the same time; a signpost carries its own weight, wind shear, bending and torsion. This week pulls together everything from Weeks 1–5: cut a section through the point you care about, work out every internal load acting there, find the stress each one produces, and add them up. That single recipe — superposition — handles any general loading.

General loading: four formulas, one method

Everything we need already exists. Each type of internal load produces stress through a formula we have derived in earlier weeks:

$$\underbrace{\sigma = \frac{F}{A}}_{\text{normal (W1)}} \qquad \underbrace{\tau = \frac{Tr}{J}}_{\text{torsion (W2)}} \qquad \underbrace{\sigma = \frac{My}{I}}_{\text{bending (W3)}} \qquad \underbrace{\tau = \frac{VQ}{It}}_{\text{shear (W5)}}$$

Because the material is linear elastic, the stress at any point under several simultaneous loads is simply the sum of the stresses each load would produce on its own. Normal stresses add into a single $\sigma$; shear stresses add into a single $\tau$ — but shear directions matter, as we will see. The combined result is captured in superposition of stresses on the equation sheet.

Step 1 — cut a section and transfer the loads

To find the stress at a point, first pass a cutting plane through it, perpendicular to the member's axis. Then transfer every external force acting on the removed part onto the section: replace the loads with an equivalent force–couple system at the section's centroid. In general this produces up to six internal force resultants:

  • an axial force $P$ along the member's axis,
  • two shear forces $V_1, V_2$ in the plane of the section,
  • a torque $T$ about the axis, and
  • two bending moments $M_1, M_2$ about the section's in-plane axes.

Each transferred couple is just force × perpendicular distance from the force's original line of action to the centroid. For statically supported members (like the crankshaft example below) you may need equilibrium of the whole body first to find the reactions, then take the section.

Watch out Be careful which moments are torques and which are bending moments — it depends entirely on the member's axis at the cut. In the pipe-assembly problem (8.37) the same applied force generates a torque about one segment's axis and a bending moment about another's.

Step 2 — stress at the point from each resultant

Now evaluate the stress each internal load produces at the specific point in question — not the maximum somewhere else on the section:

  • Axial $P$: uniform normal stress $\sigma = P/A$ everywhere on the section (normal stress).
  • Bending $M$: linear normal stress $\sigma = My/I$, where $y$ is the point's distance from that moment's neutral axis. A point on the neutral axis feels nothing from that moment (flexure formula).
  • Torque $T$: shear stress $\tau = Tr/J$, maximum at the outer surface and directed tangent to the perimeter, circulating with the torque (torsional shear).
  • Shear $V$: shear stress $\tau = VQ/It$, where $Q$ is the first moment of the area beyond the point. Zero at the free surfaces (where $Q=0$) and largest near the neutral axis — the opposite pattern to bending (transverse shear).

Recognising the zeros before computing saves enormous effort. In the crankshaft example the points on the horizontal diameter ($J$, $L$) sit on the bending neutral axis, so the moment contributes nothing there, while the points on the vertical diameter ($H$, $K$) have no transverse-shear contribution zero'd — instead, for a vertical load, it is the side points that carry $Q=0$ planes. Always ask, load by load: is this point on the neutral axis? At a free surface? On a cut where $A'=0$?

Q for circular sections

Combined-loading problems live on shafts, so the first moment of a half-circle comes up constantly. For a solid section of radius $r$, the half-area is $\tfrac12\pi r^2$ and its centroid sits $4r/3\pi$ from the axis, giving

$$Q_{\text{NA}} = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^{2}\cdot\frac{4r}{3\pi} = \frac{2r^{3}}{3}$$

For a hollow tube, subtract the inner semicircle from the outer:

$$Q = Q_{\text{outer}} - Q_{\text{inner}} = \frac{2}{3}\left(r_o^{3}-r_i^{3}\right)$$

Both are on the sheet as Q for circular sections. The width $t$ in $VQ/It$ is the full diameter for a solid shaft cut at the neutral axis, or twice the wall thickness for a tube.

Step 3 — superpose and sketch the stress element

Add the contributions. Normal stresses from $P/A$ and every $My/I$ term combine algebraically (tension positive) into one $\sigma$. Shear stresses need more care: the torsional shear circulates around the section while the transverse shear points with $V$, so at one edge of a diameter they add and at the opposite edge they subtract. In the crankshaft example the torsional 43 MPa combines with the 3.51 MPa of transverse shear to give 46.5 MPa at the bottom point and 39.5 MPa at the top.

Finish by drawing the little square stress element at the point, with the resultant $\sigma$ on its faces and the resultant $\tau$ around its sides. This picture is not decoration — it is exactly the input that stress transformation and Mohr's circle (Week 7) take to find principal stresses and the worst-case plane.

Sign discipline The safest bookkeeping is physical, not algebraic: sketch each stress's direction on the element as you compute it, then combine arrows. Trying to carry global sign conventions through four different formulas is where marks go to die.

Worked examples

Crankshaft (sample problem): a 2 kN horizontal force on the crank pin. Whole-body equilibrium gives the bearing reactions (1 kN each) and the balancing torque $T = 2\times0.045 = 0.09$ kN·m. At the section 62 mm from bearing $B$ the resultants are a 1 kN shear, a 90 N·m torque and a 62 N·m bending moment. Torsion gives 43 MPa at all four points; bending gives 59.3 MPa at the two points off the neutral axis; transverse shear gives 3.51 MPa at the two points on it, using $Q = 2r^3/3 = 887$ mm³.

Bar with three forces (8.45): a 200 kN axial compression plus two transverse forces. Section through $a$, $b$, $c$; the resultants are $P = 200$ kN, two shears and two moments. Each point collects a different subset: $c$ sits on the free edge (both shear terms zero) but far from the $M_2$ neutral axis, ending at 62.9 MPa compression with no shear at all.

Billboard (8.39): weight 36 kN and wind 12 kN acting at the panel centre, transferred 0.9 m and 3.3 m to the tube section at $H$: $P$, $V$, $T = 10.8$ kN·m and two bending moments all at once. Point $H$ lies on one moment's neutral axis, and the hollow-section $Q$ feeds the shear term. Note the tutorial's observation that the torsional and transverse shears point the same way at $H$, so they add.

The tutorial set (8.31, 8.34–8.36, 8.37, 8.41, 8.45, 8.53, 8.57, 8.69, 8.70) is all variations on the same three steps. In 8.41 the axle carries only torque and one bending moment — recognising that there is no shear, no axial force and no second moment at the section is most of the solution.

Where this leads The stress element you build here is the starting point for Week 7: transformation of stress rotates that element to find principal stresses and maximum shear, and Mohr's circle draws the whole picture at once.
