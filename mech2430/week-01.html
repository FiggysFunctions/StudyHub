Stress, Strain & Elasticity

The foundations of the whole subject: how we measure the internal forces in a loaded solid (stress), how we measure its deformation (strain), and the elastic law that ties the two together. Everything in later weeks — torsion, bending, pressure vessels, failure — is built on these ideas.

Force and normal stress

Start with a pin-jointed frame. Statics gives the member forces easily enough — say $F_{AB} = -40\ \text{kN}$ (negative meaning compression) and $F_{BC} = 50\ \text{kN}$. But that single number per member hides a lot: the same simple structure contains axial stress in the links, shear stress in the pins, and bearing stress where each pin presses against its hole. Statics tells you the force; this course is about what that force does inside the material.

When a body carries a load, the external forces are transmitted through the material as internal forces distributed over every cross-section. Stress is the intensity — the concentration — of that internal force, so that parts of different sizes can be compared. Each element of area $dA$ carries a force $dF = \sigma\,dA$, and integrating recovers the total:

$$\sigma = \frac{dF}{dA} \qquad F = \int_A \sigma\,dA \qquad \sigma_{\text{avg}} = \frac{P}{A}$$

If the force acts perpendicular to the cut surface it produces a normal stress. By convention tension is positive and compression negative. The SI unit is the pascal ($1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2$); in engineering we almost always work in megapascals, $1\ \text{MPa} = 1\ \text{N/mm}^2$, which is convenient because dimensions are usually in millimetres. See the normal-stress relation on the equation sheet.

The two ideas the whole course rests on (1) Failure is governed by the maximum stress, not the average. $P/A$ is only an average, and the real distribution across a section is rarely uniform — so MECH2430 is about distributions, not averages. (2) Normal and shear stress are two sides of the same coin. They are not independent quantities; as the oblique-plane section below shows, pure tension on one plane is shear on another. Learning to combine and compare them is what the failure theories later in the course are for.

Shear and bearing stress

When the internal force acts tangent to the surface instead of perpendicular to it, it produces a shear stress, written $\tau$. The average shear stress over an area $A$ carrying a tangential force $V$ is

$$\tau_{\text{avg}} = \frac{V}{A}$$

Shear shows up wherever material is being "scissored": pins, bolts, rivets and glued laps. A pin in single shear carries the whole load across one cross-section; in double shear the load is split across two, halving the stress.

A closely related quantity is bearing stress — the contact pressure pressed between two bodies, such as a bolt shank against the side of its hole. It is computed on the projected contact area rather than the true curved surface:

$$\sigma_b = \frac{P}{A_b} = \frac{P}{t\,d}$$

Like $P/A$, both of these are averages — the true shear distribution across a pin and the true pressure around the inside of a hole both vary considerably. Full forms: average shear & bearing stress.

Watch out Bearing area is the projected rectangle (diameter × thickness) for a pin in a plate, not the curved area of the hole. Mixing these up is a classic exam slip.

Stress on an oblique plane

Stress is not a single number attached to a point — it depends on the orientation of the plane you choose to look at. Take an axially loaded bar and cut it on a plane whose normal makes an angle $\theta$ with the axis. The same internal force now acts over a larger, tilted area, and it resolves into both a normal and a shear component:

The axial force $P$ resolves into a component normal to the plane, $F = P\cos\theta$, and one along it, $V = P\sin\theta$. Both act over the enlarged area $A_\theta = A_0/\cos\theta$, so:

$$\sigma_\theta = \frac{P}{A_0}\cos^2\theta \qquad \tau_\theta = \frac{P}{A_0}\sin\theta\cos\theta$$

Two lessons follow. First, the normal stress is largest on the transverse plane ($\theta = 0$) while the shear stress peaks at $\theta = 45^\circ$, where it reaches $\sigma/2$ — which is why ductile bars often fail on $45^\circ$ slip planes. Second, because the answer depends on orientation, a complete description of stress at a point needs more than one number. That motivates the oblique-plane view and, ultimately, the stress tensor below. Full form: stress on an oblique plane.

Coming back later This one calculation explains two results further into the course: why a twisted piece of chalk breaks on a $45^\circ$ helix (Week 2), and why the Tresca failure criterion compares $\tau_{\max}$ against $\sigma_y/2$ rather than $\sigma_y$.

Three-dimensional stress and the stress tensor

Take a general body, cut a plane parallel to the $y$–$z$ axes, and look at an element of area $dA$ on the exposed face. The resultant force on it decomposes into one normal and two shear components, and shrinking $dA$ to a point defines three stresses on that plane:

$$\sigma_x = \frac{dF_x}{dA} \qquad \tau_{xy} = \frac{dV_{xy}}{dA} \qquad \tau_{xz} = \frac{dV_{xz}}{dA}$$

Repeating for planes normal to $y$ and $z$ gives nine components in total — the faces of an infinitesimal cube of material at that point.

So what kind of object is stress: a vector or a scalar? Neither. Stress is a rank-2 tensor — a linear map that takes one vector and returns another. Feed it the unit normal of any plane you choose and it hands back the force concentration (the traction vector) on that plane. Written as a matrix:

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{x} & \tau_{xy} & \tau_{xz} \\ \tau_{yx} & \sigma_{y} & \tau_{yz} \\ \tau_{zx} & \tau_{zy} & \sigma_{z} \end{bmatrix}$$

Two quick demonstrations make the idea concrete. Multiply by the unit normal of the plane facing the $x$-axis and you simply read off the first column — the three stresses on that face:

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_x\\ \tau_{yx}\\ \tau_{zx}\end{bmatrix}$$

Now try a plane tilted at $45^\circ$ between the $x$ and $y$ axes, whose unit normal is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)$:

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_x + \tau_{xy}\\ \tau_{yx} + \sigma_y\\ \tau_{zx} + \tau_{zy}\end{bmatrix}$$

That is the oblique-plane calculation from above, generalised — one object encoding the stress on every plane through the point at once.

Moment equilibrium of the cube forces the shear stresses to be symmetric, $\tau_{xy} = \tau_{yx}$, $\tau_{yz} = \tau_{zy}$, $\tau_{zx} = \tau_{xz}$, so only six of the nine components are independent. Finding the orientation in which all the shear terms vanish gives the principal stresses — that is an eigenvalue problem for this matrix, and it is what the stress-transformation weeks are really doing. See traction & the stress tensor.

Strain

Where stress measures internal force, strain measures deformation. Normal strain is the change in length per unit original length of a fibre:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\delta}{L} = \frac{L' - L}{L} \qquad \varepsilon = \lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{\Delta\delta}{\Delta x} = \frac{d\delta}{dx}$$

The second form is the one that matters when the cross-section varies or the load is distributed — strain is properly a local quantity, defined at a point, exactly as stress is. It is dimensionless (often quoted in microstrain, $\mu\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$). Alongside it, shear strain $\gamma$ measures the change in angle between two originally perpendicular fibres, in radians. Just like stress, the strains at a point assemble into a symmetric strain tensor. See normal strain on the equation sheet.

Notation trap — factor of two Engineering shear strain $\gamma_{xy}$ is the total change in angle, and that is what $\tau = G\gamma$ uses. The strain tensor carries half of it off the diagonal, $\varepsilon_{xy} = \tfrac12\gamma_{xy}$, so that it rotates the same way the stress tensor does. Halve your $\gamma$ before putting it into the tensor or a transformation equation — see engineering vs tensor shear strain.

The tensile test and Hooke's law

The tensile test pulls a standard specimen and records load against extension, which we convert to a stress–strain curve. For most engineering metals the first portion is straight: stress is proportional to strain. This is Hooke's law, and the slope of that line is Young's modulus $E$, a measure of stiffness:

$$\sigma = E\,\varepsilon$$

Worth carrying in your head, because tutorial problems rarely bother to state them twice:

Aluminium≈ 70≈ 25≈ 0.33

The straight region ends at the yield point; beyond it the material deforms permanently (plastically). For this course we mostly work in the linear-elastic region, treating materials as elastic–perfectly-plastic only when we deliberately study yielding. The relation $\sigma = E\varepsilon$ is the simplest constitutive law — see Hooke's law (1D).

Axial deformation

Combining the definitions of stress, strain and Hooke's law gives the stretch of a bar under axial load. For a prismatic member of length $L$, area $A$ and modulus $E$ carrying internal force $N$:

$$\delta = \frac{NL}{AE}$$

If the load, area or material changes along the length, sum the contributions of each segment, $\delta = \sum \dfrac{N_i L_i}{A_i E_i}$, or integrate when they vary continuously. The group $AE$ is the axial rigidity: large $AE$ means a stiff member. Full form on the sheet: axial deformation.

Statically indeterminate members and superposition

If equilibrium alone gives enough equations to find every internal force, a problem is statically determinate. When there are more supports or members than equilibrium can resolve — a bar fixed at both ends, say — it is statically indeterminate. These are solved by adding a compatibility condition (a geometric statement about how deformations must fit together) to the equilibrium equations, then substituting the load–deformation law $\delta = NL/AE$.

A powerful aid here is superposition: because the governing relations are linear, the response to several loads equals the sum of the responses to each load applied alone. That turns the compatibility argument into a reliable four-step recipe — the superposition method:

  1. Identify the redundant reactions — the ones equilibrium cannot resolve.
  2. Remove them, and re-apply each as an unknown load on the now-determinate structure.
  3. Work out the deformations from the given loads and from the redundant loads separately.
  4. Superimpose them and impose the true support condition (usually zero net deformation) to solve for the unknowns.

The same four steps reappear throughout the course — for indeterminate shafts in Week 2, for residual stresses after yielding, and for beam deflections later on. It is worth getting fluent with it now.

Thermal effects

A temperature change makes most materials expand or contract. The free thermal strain for a change $\Delta T$ is

$$\delta_T = \alpha\,\Delta T\,L$$

where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of thermal expansion. If the member is free to move, this produces deformation but no stress. If it is restrained, the prevented expansion shows up as thermal stress — and this is the cleanest possible illustration of the superposition method above. Take a bar fixed at both ends, release the reaction at $B$, let it expand freely, then apply $R_B$ as an unknown load that squeezes it back:

$$\delta_T + \delta_{R_B} = \alpha(\Delta T)L + \frac{R_B L}{AE} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; R_B = -AE\,\alpha(\Delta T), \qquad \sigma = -E\,\alpha(\Delta T)$$

Notice what dropped out: both $L$ and $A$ cancel from the stress. A fully restrained bar develops the same thermal stress whether it is 10 mm or 10 m long, and making it thicker does not help at all — which is why expansion joints, not bigger sections, are how bridges and pipelines deal with temperature. The negative sign says heating a restrained bar puts it in compression. See thermal deformation and thermal stress.

Worth doing yourself Put numbers in and the result is startling. A fully restrained steel bar ($E = 210\ \text{GPa}$, $\alpha \approx 11.7\times10^{-6}\,/^{\circ}\text{C}$) warmed by just $50^{\circ}\text{C}$: $\sigma = -(210\times10^{9})(11.7\times10^{-6})(50) \approx -123\ \text{MPa}$ — around half the yield stress of mild steel, from a temperature change you would barely notice. This is why thermal effects are never an afterthought in design.

Poisson's ratio

Stretch a bar lengthwise and it gets thinner across. Poisson's ratio $\nu$ is the (positive) ratio of lateral contraction to axial extension:

$$\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{lat}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{long}}}$$

For metals $\nu$ sits around $0.3$; the thermodynamic limit for isotropic materials is $0 \le \nu \le 0.5$, with $\nu = 0.5$ meaning incompressible (rubber-like). See Poisson's ratio.

Generalised Hooke's law

In three dimensions a normal stress in one direction strains the material in all three — directly along its own axis, and through Poisson contraction along the other two. Superposing the three normal stresses gives the generalised Hooke's law for an isotropic material:

$$\varepsilon_x = \frac{1}{E}\big[\sigma_x - \nu(\sigma_y + \sigma_z)\big]$$

with matching expressions for $\varepsilon_y$ and $\varepsilon_z$ by cycling the subscripts. The shear components are independent and uncoupled, each governed by the shear modulus:

$$\gamma_{xy} = \frac{\tau_{xy}}{G}, \quad \gamma_{yz} = \frac{\tau_{yz}}{G}, \quad \gamma_{zx} = \frac{\tau_{zx}}{G}$$

Shear modulus

The shear modulus $G$ plays the role of $E$ for shear, relating shear stress to shear strain by $\tau = G\gamma$. For an isotropic material the three elastic constants are not independent — they are linked by

$$G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}$$

so any two of $E$, $G$, $\nu$ fix the third — an isotropic material has only two independent elastic constants, not three. A $\tau$–$\gamma$ plot looks much like a $\sigma$–$\varepsilon$ plot, with its own linear region and yield point; for most engineering materials the strength in shear is roughly half the strength in tension, which is the empirical fact behind the Tresca failure criterion later in the course. See shear form of Hooke's law and relation between elastic constants.

Dilation and bulk modulus

The fractional change in volume of a stressed element is its dilation, equal to the sum of the three normal strains:

$$e = \varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y + \varepsilon_z = \frac{1-2\nu}{E}\,(\sigma_x + \sigma_y + \sigma_z)$$

Under uniform pressure $p$ (so $\sigma_x=\sigma_y=\sigma_z=-p$) this defines the bulk modulus $K$, the resistance to volume change:

$$K = \frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)}$$

Notice that as $\nu \to 0.5$ the dilation vanishes and $K \to \infty$ — the material becomes incompressible, confirming the upper limit on Poisson's ratio. Full forms: dilation and bulk modulus.
