Bending

How beams carry transverse loads. We build the elastic flexure formula from the geometry of a bent beam, learn to find the second moment of area of real cross-sections, then extend the ideas to composite beams, to sections that have yielded, and to loads applied off the centroid. The whole chapter runs on one principle: strain comes from geometry, stress comes from the material — and superposition ties everything together.

From transverse loading to pure bending

Bending occurs whenever a member carries transverse or eccentric loads — the loads try to curve it rather than simply stretch it. A general transverse load produces both a shear force and a bending moment that vary along the beam. To make the problem tractable we first study the clean reference case of pure bending: a segment carrying a constant moment with no shear force (a flat region of the shear diagram). Everything else is then handled by superposition of pure-bending results.

For a beam segment in pure bending, summing the internal stresses over the cross-section must reproduce the applied couple and nothing else:

$$\int_A \sigma_x\,dA = 0, \qquad \int_A z\,\sigma_x\,dA = 0, \qquad \int_A -y\,\sigma_x\,dA = M$$

These three conditions are not enough on their own — the problem is statically indeterminate — so, exactly as with axial load and torsion, we have to look at the deformation.

Deformation, the neutral axis and bending strain

Assume the beam bends into a circular arc of radius of curvature $\rho$, and that plane cross-sections stay plane. Fibres on the outside of the bend stretch, fibres on the inside shorten, and somewhere in between is a surface that neither stretches nor shortens — the neutral axis. Measuring $y$ from that axis, the arc length of a fibre gives a strain that is purely geometric:

$$\varepsilon_x = -\frac{y}{\rho}$$

So strain varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis, with the maximum at the extreme fibre. This result depends only on geometry, so it holds whether or not the material has yielded — a fact we lean on heavily later. See bending strain.

Elastic behaviour and the flexure formula

In the elastic range Hooke's law converts the linear strain into a linear stress, $\sigma_x = E\varepsilon_x = -\tfrac{y}{\rho}E$. Substituting into the force condition $\int_A \sigma_x\,dA = 0$ gives $\int_A y\,dA = 0$, which is exactly the statement that the neutral axis passes through the centroid of the section. Substituting the same stress into the moment condition and collecting the geometry into a single integral gives the flexure formula:

$$\sigma_x = \frac{My}{I}, \qquad I = \int_A y^2\,dA$$

The integral $I$ is the second moment of area about the neutral axis. It is the single geometric number that sets how stiff and how strong a section is in bending — putting material far from the neutral axis (deep sections, I-beams) makes $I$ large.

Second moment of area and the parallel-axis theorem

For a rectangle of width $b$ and depth $d$ about its own centroid, integrating $y^2$ gives the result you will use constantly:

$$I_{\text{rect}} = \frac{bd^3}{12}$$

Real sections — H-shapes, channels, I-beams — are assembled from rectangles. To combine them, shift each piece's second moment from its own centroid onto the section's neutral axis with the parallel-axis theorem:

$$I = I_0 + A d^2$$

Two habits pay off in the tutorial problems. For a symmetric H- or I-section you can often take a big outer rectangle and subtract the two side notches. For an unsymmetric channel you must first locate the centroid ($\bar y = \sum A_i y_i / \sum A_i$) before applying the parallel-axis theorem — the neutral axis is not at mid-height.

Watch out A rectangle's second moment goes as depth cubed: $bd^3/12$ about the axis parallel to the width. Bending a section about the "hard" axis (large depth) versus the "easy" axis changes $I$ dramatically — Prob. 4.5 vs 4.6 is exactly this comparison on the same H-section.

Curvature and flexural rigidity

The deformation of a beam is captured by the curvature $1/\rho$ of its neutral axis. Combining $\varepsilon_{\max}=Y/\rho$ with $\sigma_{\max}=MY/I$ and Hooke's law gives

$$\frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{M}{EI}$$

The product $EI$ is the flexural rigidity — the bending analogue of axial rigidity $EA$ and torsional rigidity $GJ$. A large $EI$ means the beam stays straight under load. See curvature / beam deflection. (In Prob. 4.22 this relation runs backwards: a rod coiled to a known radius has its stress and moment fixed by $\rho$.)

Transverse (anticlastic) deformation

Bending also deforms the cross-section sideways. Fibres above the neutral axis are in compression, so by Poisson's ratio they expand across the width; fibres below stretch axially and contract across the width. The section therefore curves gently in the opposite sense to the main bending — a saddle shape called anticlastic curvature, with radius

$$\frac{1}{\rho'} = \frac{\nu}{\rho}$$

Full form on the sheet: anticlastic curvature.

Composite beams and transformed sections

Everything up to Hooke's law holds regardless of material, so for a composite beam (timber bolted to an aluminium plate, reinforced concrete, laminates) strain is still linear across the whole section — but stress jumps at the material boundary because $E$ jumps. The trick is to transform the section into an equivalent one made of a single reference material using the modular ratio

$$n = \frac{E_2}{E_1}$$

Replace the second material with a strip of the reference material that is $n$ times as wide (never taller — the depth carries the strain distribution and must not change). Analyse the all-one-material transformed section with the ordinary flexure formula, then recover the true stress in the transformed region by multiplying the computed stress by $n$:

$$\sigma_2 = n\,\sigma_{\text{transf}}$$

The neutral axis passes through the centroid of the transformed section. In Question 3 the stiff aluminium ($E=73$ GPa) becomes a much wider band of timber ($E=8$ GPa) with $n\approx 9.1$; the same physical strain then produces roughly nine times the stress in the aluminium. See transformed section.

Elastoplastic bending

Load a beam past first yield and the outer fibres reach $\sigma_y$ while a strip near the neutral axis is still elastic — an elastic core. Strain is still linear (geometry), but the stress distribution flattens to $\pm\sigma_y$ in the yielded zones. The moment at which yield first occurs comes straight from the flexure formula:

$$M_y = \frac{\sigma_y I}{Y}$$

Beyond that, integrate the actual stress block. For a general symmetric section, treat the elastic core through its own $I$ and each fully plastic zone as a resultant force $\sigma_y A$ acting at its centroid:

$$M = \sigma_y\!\left(\frac{I_{\text{elastic}}}{y_y} + \sum_{\text{plastic}} A\bar{y}\right)$$

For the special case of a rectangle this integrates to a closed form:

$$M = \tfrac{3}{2}M_y\!\left(1 - \tfrac13\frac{y_y^{2}}{Y^{2}}\right)$$

As the elastic core vanishes the moment approaches the fully plastic moment $M_p$. The ratio $M_p/M_y$ is the shape factor, which depends only on shape — exactly $1.5$ for a rectangle, less for an I-beam whose area already sits far from the neutral axis. See moment at first yield and elastoplastic bending.

Watch out The tidy $\tfrac32 M_y$ formula is for rectangles only. For the I-beam in Prob. 4.74 you cannot use it — build the fully plastic moment from flange and web resultants directly.

Residual stress and curvature

When the moment is removed, unloading happens elastically — the material springs back along a straight $\sigma = My/I$ line using the same $M$. The leftover locked-in state is the loading distribution minus that elastic unloading distribution:

$$\sigma_{\text{resid}} = \sigma_{\text{load}} - \sigma_{\text{unload}}$$

This residual stress is self-equilibrating, and the beam keeps a permanent curvature (springback). The permanent radius follows from the difference of curvatures:

$$\frac{1}{\rho_{\text{resid}}} = \frac{1}{\rho_L} - \frac{1}{\rho_{UL}}$$

This is the direct analogue of the permanent twist left in a shaft loaded past yield in Week 2. Full form: residual curvature.

Eccentric axial loading

Finally, superposition lets us handle a load applied off the centroid. An axial force $P$ at eccentricity $d$ is statically equivalent to a centric force $P$ plus a couple $M = Pd$. Add the uniform axial stress and the linear bending stress:

$$\sigma_x = \frac{P}{A} - \frac{My}{I}$$

This is the model for clamps, hooks and offset columns — Prob. 4.113 (the G-clamp) is worked exactly this way. See eccentric axial loading.

Worked examples

Three from this week's tutorial set, with the setups paraphrased and the method shown. Full solutions are in the uploaded tutorial PDFs.

Curvature fixes the stress (4.22). Straight 6 mm-diameter rods, 30 m long, are stored coiled inside a drum of 1.25 m inside diameter ($E = 200$ GPa). This runs the flexure relations backwards: the geometry fixes $\rho$, and everything else follows. The rod centreline sits one rod-radius inside the drum, $\rho = 0.625 - 0.003 = 0.622\ \text{m}$, so from $\varepsilon = c/\rho$ and Hooke's law:

$$\sigma_{\max} = \frac{Ec}{\rho} = \frac{(200\times10^{9})(0.003)}{0.622} \approx 965\ \text{MPa}, \qquad M = \frac{EI}{\rho} \approx 20.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$$

using $I = \pi c^4/4 = 6.36\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}^4$. Note how little moment it takes — but how enormous the stress is. Coiling thin rod is a severe operation, and the problem's caveat "assuming the yield strength is not exceeded" is doing a lot of work.

Composite timber–aluminium beam (Question 3). Two 100 mm × 300 mm timber planks ($E = 8$ GPa) are bolted either side of a 25 mm × 300 mm aluminium plate ($E = 73$ GPa), spanning 4 m simply supported with a 15 kN central load, so $M = PL/4 = 15\ \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$. Transform to all-timber with $n = 73/8 = 9.125$: the aluminium's 25 mm becomes $25 \times 9.125 = 228\ \text{mm}$ of timber, giving a transformed width of $100+228+100 = 428\ \text{mm}$ at the same 300 mm depth. Then

$$I = \frac{bd^3}{12} = 9.63\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^4 \qquad \sigma_{\text{timber}} = \frac{Mc}{I} \approx 2.34\ \text{MPa} \qquad \sigma_{\text{Al}} = n\,\sigma_{\text{transf}} \approx 21.3\ \text{MPa}$$

and the curvature from the reference material, $\rho = E_{\text{timber}}I/M \approx 514\ \text{m}$. The aluminium carries roughly nine times the stress of the timber beside it — same strain, stiffer material.

Elastoplastic rectangle (4.42). A 50 mm × 120 mm section carries $M = 36.8\ \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$, with $\sigma_y = 240$ MPa and $E = 200$ GPa. First check whether it has yielded at all: $I = bd^3/12 = 7.2\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$ gives $M_y = \sigma_y I/c = 28.8\ \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$, and $36.8 > 28.8$, so an elastic core exists. Invert the rectangular elastoplastic formula:

$$36.8 = \tfrac32(28.8)\left(1 - \tfrac13\frac{y_y^2}{Y^2}\right) \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{y_y}{Y} = \tfrac23, \quad y_y = 40\ \text{mm}$$

so the elastic core is $2y_y = 80\ \text{mm}$ thick. For the curvature, the fibre at $y_y$ is exactly at yield, so $\varepsilon_y = \sigma_y/E = 1.2\times10^{-3}$ and

$$\rho = \frac{y_y}{\varepsilon_y} = \frac{0.040}{1.2\times10^{-3}} \approx 33.3\ \text{m}$$
Always check first Before reaching for any elastoplastic formula, compute $M_y$ and compare. If $M \le M_y$ the section is still fully elastic and the plain flexure formula applies — using the plastic equations there will give you nonsense.
Where this leads Bending completes the trio of basic load cases — axial (Week 1), torsion (Week 2), bending (Week 3) — and all three share the same skeleton: linear strain from geometry, stress from the material, and superposition for everything else. Next we add the shear force that accompanies real transverse loads. Quiz 1 (Week 4) covers Weeks 1–3, and the flexure formula and second moment of area are the most heavily tested part.
