Transformation of Stress & Strain

Week 6 gave us the stress at a point by superposition — but the answer came attached to whatever axes we happened to cut along. This week asks the deeper question: given $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$, $\tau_{xy}$ on one set of axes, what does the same stress state look like on rotated axes? The answer is the transformation equations, their graphical form (Mohr's circle), and the special orientations that matter most: principal planes and maximum-shear planes. The same machinery then transfers to strain, which is what we can actually measure.

Plane stress

Real components carry combined loading — normal loads, torsion, bending, transverse shear — and by the principle of superposition the stresses simply add. The question that remains is how different kinds of stress combine at a point, and we start by considering a material stressed in only two dimensions. This condition is plane stress:

$$\sigma_z = \tau_{xz} = \tau_{yz} = 0$$

It holds on any free surface and through thin plates — and it is exactly the state a Week-6 calculation leaves you with: a little square element carrying $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$ and $\tau_{xy}$.

Transformation of plane stress

Cut the element on a plane whose normal makes an angle $\theta$ with the $x$-axis and sum forces on the resulting wedge (each stress times its face area). Solving the two equilibrium equations and tidying with double-angle identities gives the stress-transformation equations:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_{x'} &= \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} + \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta\\[4pt] \sigma_{y'} &= \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} - \frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta - \tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta\\[4pt] \tau_{x'y'} &= -\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\sin 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\cos 2\theta \end{aligned}$$

Two structural features are worth memorising. The expression for $\sigma_{y'}$ is just $\sigma_{x'}$ with $\theta$ replaced by $\theta+90^\circ$, and adding them shows the sum is invariant: $\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}=\sigma_x+\sigma_y$ for every $\theta$. That invariance is a free arithmetic check on any hand calculation.

Mohr's circle

The transformation equations are parametric equations of a circle. Eliminate $\theta$ (square and add) to get

$$\left(\sigma_{x'}-\sigma_{\text{ave}}\right)^2 + \tau_{x'y'}^2 = R^2,\qquad \sigma_{\text{ave}} = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2},\qquad R = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^{2}+\tau_{xy}^{2}}$$

— a circle of centre $(\sigma_{\text{ave}},0)$ and radius $R$ in the $(\sigma,\tau)$ plane. Mohr's circle represents all the different ways of viewing the same stress state: every point on it is the normal-and-shear pair on some plane through the material point. See Mohr's circle on the equation sheet.

Two rules make it work in practice:

Angles are doubled. Coordinate directions an angle $\theta$ apart in the material sit $2\theta$ apart on the circle. Rotating the element $30^\circ$ anticlockwise moves its stress points $60^\circ$ around the circle.

Note the polarity. The sign tip used in this course: counter-clockwise shear is positive and is plotted below the line; clockwise is negative and plotted above. So plot the $x$-face point at $(\sigma_x, \tau_{xy})$ and the $y$-face point at $(\sigma_y, \tau_{yx})$ with signs assigned that way — the two points are diametrically opposite, and the line joining them fixes the circle.

Exam workflow From $(\sigma_x,\tau)$ and $(\sigma_y,\tau)$: centre $\bar\sigma=(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)/2$, radius $R$ by Pythagoras on the half-difference and the shear. Then $\sigma_1=\bar\sigma+R$, $\sigma_2=\bar\sigma-R$, and $\tan 2\theta_p$ from the little triangle. For a rotated element, step $2\theta$ around the circle and read off $\sigma_{x'}=\bar\sigma\pm R\sin/\cos(\cdot)$ as the geometry dictates — exactly the Week-7 worked solutions.

Or… work with the stress tensor

There is a slicker route using the definition of stress as a tensor from Week 1. In 2D,

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{xx} & \tau_{xy}\\ \tau_{xy} & \sigma_{yy}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \frac{d\tilde f}{dA}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\tilde n$$

The stress tensor maps the unit normal $\tilde n$ of any plane to the traction — the force concentration — acting on that plane. Dot the traction back onto the normal and tangent unit vectors to split it into components:

$$\sigma_{x'x'} = \tilde n^{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\tilde n,\qquad \tau_{x'y'} = \tilde t^{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\tilde n,\qquad \sigma_{y'y'} = \tilde t^{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\tilde t$$

with $\tilde n=(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)$ and $\tilde t=(-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta)$. Three small matrix products reproduce the transformation equations exactly — often the fastest hand method for a single angle. See traction & the stress tensor.

Principal planes and principal stresses

The extreme normal stresses sit where the circle crosses the $\sigma$-axis — where shear is zero. These are the principal stresses, acting on the principal planes:

$$\sigma_{\max,\min} = \frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^{2}+\tau_{xy}^{2}} = \sigma_{\text{ave}}\pm R, \qquad \tan 2\theta_p = \frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}$$

In tensor language this has a beautiful reading: a principal plane is one where the traction is purely normal — a scalar multiple of the normal vector, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}\tilde n_p = \sigma_p\,\tilde n_p$. That is an eigenvalue problem: principal stresses are the eigenvalues of the stress tensor, principal directions its eigenvectors. See principal stresses.

Maximum in-plane shear

The largest in-plane shear stress is the top (and bottom) of the circle — the radius itself — reached on planes $45^\circ$ from the principal planes ($2\theta = 90^\circ$ on the circle). The normal stress there is not zero but $\sigma_{\text{ave}}$:

$$\tau_{\max,\text{in-plane}} = R = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^{2}+\tau_{xy}^{2}},\qquad \theta_s = \theta_p + 45^\circ$$

See maximum in-plane shear — and note the qualifier "in-plane", because of what comes next.

3D Mohr's circles and the out-of-plane trap

"It can be shown" that rotating an element about any one of its principal axes behaves exactly like plane stress, which leads to the three-dimensional picture: three Mohr's circles drawn between the three principal stresses, ordered by convention

$$\sigma_3 \le \sigma_2 \le \sigma_1$$

All achievable $(\sigma,\tau)$ states lie between the circles; the true maximum shear is the radius of the biggest circle:

$$\tau_{\max} = \frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_3}{2}$$

Here is the trap. In plane stress the $z$-axis is automatically a principal axis with principal stress zero, so there are three cases (see max shear in 3D):

$$\begin{aligned} 0 < \sigma_2 < \sigma_1 &\quad\Rightarrow\quad \tau_{\max}=\tfrac{\sigma_1}{2}\ \text{(out of plane)}\\[2pt] \sigma_3 < 0 < \sigma_1 &\quad\Rightarrow\quad \tau_{\max}=\tfrac{\sigma_1-\sigma_3}{2}\ \text{(in plane)}\\[2pt] \sigma_3 < \sigma_2 < 0 &\quad\Rightarrow\quad \tau_{\max}=\tfrac{|\sigma_3|}{2}\ \text{(out of plane)} \end{aligned}$$
Watch out If both in-plane principal stresses come out positive, what you have been calling "$\sigma_2$" is really the middle stress and the zero out-of-plane stress is $\sigma_3$. The maximum shear then lives on a plane rotated $45^\circ$ about an in-plane axis, and equals $\sigma_1/2$ — bigger than the in-plane radius $R$. This exact step appears in the Fig. 7.13 and Fig. 7.34 tutorial problems, and it is what the Tresca criterion needs later.

Plane strain and strain transformation

By analogy with plane stress, plane strain means no strain in planes normal to $z$:

$$\varepsilon_z = \gamma_{xz} = \gamma_{yz} = 0$$

Strain transforms between coordinate systems in exactly the same pattern as stress, with one crucial substitution: $\gamma/2$ plays the role of $\tau$ (these are not numerically the same equations as before!):

$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{x'} &= \frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\sin 2\theta\\[4pt] \frac{\gamma_{x'y'}}{2} &= -\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\sin 2\theta + \frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\cos 2\theta \end{aligned}$$

So there is a Mohr's circle for plane strain too, with centre $\varepsilon_{\text{ave}}=(\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y)/2$ and radius $R=\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}{2}\right)^2+\left(\tfrac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\right)^2}$, giving principal strains and their directions.

Question to ponder Are principal stresses and principal strains in the same directions? For an isotropic material, yes — Hooke's law couples normal strains only to normal stresses, so a shear-free orientation for stress is shear-free for strain too.

Measuring strain: gauges and rosettes

Stress is difficult to measure (unless you only want an average); strain is easy. A strain gauge is a zig-zag wire bonded to the surface: stretch it and Poisson's ratio thins the wire, its resistance rises, and a Wheatstone bridge reads the change with great sensitivity. But one gauge only measures normal strain along its own axis.

To recover the full in-plane strain state, a strain rosette mounts two or three gauges at known angles ($90^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ patterns are standard). Write the first strain-transformation equation for each gauge direction:

$$\varepsilon_i = \varepsilon_x\cos^2\theta_i + \varepsilon_y\sin^2\theta_i + \gamma_{xy}\sin\theta_i\cos\theta_i \qquad (i = 1,2,3)$$

and solve the three equations simultaneously for $\varepsilon_x$, $\varepsilon_y$, $\gamma_{xy}$ — or go straight to principal strains if you prefer. Tutorial problem 7.144 (the $15^\circ/30^\circ/45^\circ$ rosette) is exactly this. See strain rosette.

The Week 7 problem patterns

The tutorial and worked solutions rehearse three recurring patterns:

Pure transformation (Probs 7.5–7.13): given a stress element, find principal planes/stresses, a rotated element, or maximum shear. Draw the circle, find $\bar\sigma$ and $R$, read everything off — then sketch the answer as a rotated stress element with the angle marked.

Combined loading first, transformation second (Probs 7.24–7.26, 7.53: the lever, post and axle problems): the Week-6 recipe produces $\sigma$ and $\tau$ at the point — internal resultants at the cut, then $P/A$, $My/I$, $Tr/J$, $VQ/It$ superposed — and this week's machinery converts that element into principal stresses and maximum shear. This is the full pipeline Quiz 2 examines.

The out-of-plane check (Probs 7.66, 7.69): "consider both in-plane and out-of-plane shearing stresses" — apply the three-case rule above before declaring $\tau_{\max}$.
