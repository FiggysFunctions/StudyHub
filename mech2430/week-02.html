Torsion

Last week we stretched and compressed; this week we twist. A torque applied about the axis of a shaft produces a distribution of shear stress across its cross-section. The theory tracks axial loading almost line for line — a strain that grows with position, Hooke's law, a section property, and a deflection formula — so lean on that analogy throughout. The new ingredients are the polar moment of area and, in place of a stretch, an angle of twist.

Torque and shear stress

Consider a small patch of area $dA$ on the cross-section of a shaft carrying a torque $T$. The shear stress $\tau$ acting on it contributes a small force $dF = \tau\,dA$, and because that force acts at a radius $r$ from the axis it contributes a moment $r\,dF$ about it. Summing over the whole section, the internal torque is the moment of the shear-stress distribution:

$$T = \int_A r\,\tau\,dA$$

By the same argument used for a scissored pin last week, an equal shear stress must act on the longitudinal planes of the shaft as well as the transverse one — shear stress always comes in complementary pairs. Knowing $T$ alone is not enough to find $\tau$: the equation above is one relation between a whole distribution of unknown stresses, so like the indeterminate problems of Week 1 we must look at the deformation to make progress.

Twist geometry and shear strain

For a circular shaft a crucial simplification holds: every cross-section stays plane and simply rotates, like the cards in a twisted deck. If the far end rotates through an angle of twist $\phi$ over a length $L$, a fibre at radius $r$ is sheared through

$$\gamma = \frac{r\phi}{L}, \qquad \gamma_{\max} = \frac{R\phi}{L}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{r}{R}\,\gamma_{\max}$$

so the shear strain grows linearly from zero at the centre to a maximum at the surface. This is purely geometric — no material law yet. It is worth stressing that it relies on the circular symmetry: a non-circular shaft warps out of plane when twisted, and these simple relations no longer apply. See shear strain in torsion.

The torsion formula

Below yield, Hooke's law in shear ($\tau = G\gamma$) carries the linear strain distribution straight into a linear stress distribution, $\tau = (r/R)\,\tau_{\max}$. Substituting that into $T = \int_A r\,\tau\,dA$ and pulling the constants out of the integral gives the central result of the week:

$$\tau = \frac{Tr}{J}, \qquad \tau_{\max} = \frac{TR}{J}, \qquad J = \int_A r^2\,dA$$

The quantity $J$ is the polar moment of area — the torsional counterpart of the second moment of area $I$ from bending. Taking a thin annular slice $dA = 2\pi r\,dr$ and integrating gives the standard results

$$J_{\text{solid}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi R^4, \qquad J_{\text{hollow}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi\big(R_o^{4} - R_i^{4}\big)$$

Keep $J = \int r^2\,dA$ (polar moment of area, units m$^4$) distinct from both the second moment of area $I = \int y^2\,dA$ and the mass moment of inertia $\int r^2\,dm$, which share the name "moment of inertia" but not the meaning. Full form: torsional shear stress.

Angle of twist

Rearranging the surface relation $\gamma_{\max} = R\phi/L$ together with $\gamma_{\max} = \tau_{\max}/G = TR/JG$ and cancelling $R$ gives the angle through which the shaft twists:

$$\phi = \int_0^L \frac{T}{JG}\,dx = \frac{TL}{JG}$$

This is the exact mirror of last week's $\delta = NL/AE$. The group $GJ$ is the torsional rigidity — a large $GJ$ means a shaft that resists twisting, just as a large $AE$ resists stretching and a large $EI$ resists bending. When the torque, section or material changes along the shaft, sum the segments, $\phi = \sum T_i L_i / J_i G_i$, or integrate when they vary continuously. See angle of twist and the definition.

Why hollow shafts win Because stress and lever arm both grow with radius, the material near the axis does very little work. Hollowing a 30 mm shaft out to a 20 mm bore (Example 3.31) removes 44% of the material but makes the shaft only about 24% more flexible — nearly half the weight for a quarter the loss in stiffness. This is why drive shafts and bicycle frames are tubes.

Stress concentrations at changes in section

Everything so far assumes a smooth prismatic shaft. Real shafts are usually stepped, and the shear stress crowds around the sudden change in diameter, rising well above the value the torsion formula predicts. We account for this with a stress-concentration factor $k$ read from a design chart:

$$\tau_{\max} = k\,\frac{T(d/2)}{J}$$

The factor $k$ depends on the ratio of the fillet radius to the smaller diameter, $r/d$, for a given diameter ratio $D/d$; the sharper the step, the larger $k$. Use the smaller (downstream) diameter for $d$ and its $J$. Concentrations matter most for brittle materials and for fatigue; a ductile shaft under a steady load simply yields locally and redistributes, so $k$ is often set aside in static ductile design. Full form: torsional stress concentration.

Statically indeterminate and composite shafts

When a shaft is fixed at both ends, or built from two materials bonded together, equilibrium alone cannot divide the torque between the parts — the problem is statically indeterminate. As with axial members, we close it by adding a compatibility condition on the angle of twist and then substituting the load–deformation law $\phi = TL/JG$:

$$\underbrace{T_A + T_B = T}_{\text{equilibrium}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\phi_1 = \phi_2}_{\text{compatibility}}$$

For a single shaft of segment lengths $L_1$ and $L_2$ fixed at both ends with the torque applied at the join, these two equations solve to $T_A = T/(1 + L_1/L_2)$ and $T_B = T/(1 + L_2/L_1)$ — the reactions split in inverse proportion to the segment lengths. A composite shaft, such as a steel core inside an aluminium jacket or a spindle inside a bonded sleeve, works the same way: both parts share the same twist, so the torque divides in proportion to each part's $GJ$ and the stiffer material takes the larger share. This uses the same compatibility idea, and often superposition, that we met for indeterminate axial problems. Worked below in Examples 3.52 and P3.49.

Failure of brittle shafts

At the surface of a twisted shaft the stress state is pure shear. But stress depends on the plane you look at — exactly the oblique-plane lesson from Week 1. Resolving the pure-shear state onto a plane at angle $\theta$ produces a normal stress as well as a shear stress:

$$\sigma_\theta = \tau_{\max}\sin 2\theta \qquad \tau_\theta = \tau_{\max}\cos 2\theta$$

The normal stress reaches its maximum at $\theta = 45^\circ$, where it equals $\tau_{\max}$ in magnitude — a pure tension as large as the shear. Brittle materials such as chalk, cast iron and rock are far weaker in tension than in shear, so they crack along that $45^\circ$ plane, spiralling around the shaft to give the characteristic helical fracture. A ductile shaft, weaker in shear, instead fails on the transverse plane. This is the same oblique-plane reasoning applied to a state of pure shear.

Plastic deformation and elastoplastic shafts

The torsion formula assumes Hooke's law, so it stops being valid once the outer skin reaches the yield stress in shear. To go further we adopt the idealised elastic–perfectly-plastic model: $\tau = G\gamma$ up to $\tau_y$, then a constant $\tau = \tau_y$ however large the strain. The geometric relation $\gamma = r\phi/L$ still holds, so an over-loaded shaft develops a yielded outer ring at $\tau_y$ around an elastic core of radius $r_y$. The torque that first brings the surface to yield is

$$T_y = \frac{J\tau_y}{R} = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi R^{3}\tau_y$$

Integrating the stress over the elastic core plus the fully plastic ring gives the torque for any core radius, and hence for any angle of twist beyond first yield:

$$T = \tfrac{4}{3}T_y\!\left(1 - \tfrac14\Big(\tfrac{r_y}{R}\Big)^{3}\right) = \tfrac{4}{3}T_y\!\left(1 - \tfrac14\Big(\tfrac{\phi_y}{\phi}\Big)^{3}\right)$$

As the core shrinks to nothing ($r_y \to 0$) the shaft becomes fully plastic at $T_p = \tfrac{4}{3}T_y$ — it can never carry more than a third again its first-yield torque. See torque at first yield and elastoplastic torsion.

Residual stress and permanent twist

Once part of a shaft has yielded, releasing the torque does not return it to its original state: it is left with a permanent twist and a locked-in residual stress. The key idea is that unloading is elastic, even from a plastic state. So we find the residual state by superposition — the true elastoplastic loading distribution, minus a fictitious purely elastic unloading torque of the same magnitude ($\tau = TR/J$):

$$\tau_{\text{residual}} = \tau_{\text{load}} - \tau_{\text{unload}}, \qquad \phi_{\text{perm}} = \phi_{\text{load}} - \phi_{\text{unload}}$$

The result is a self-equilibrating stress field that opposes the direction of the original loading. In Example 3.159/3.160 a 24 mm shaft twisted to $39.3^\circ$ springs back by $33.4^\circ$ when released, leaving a permanent twist of about $5.9^\circ$ and a residual surface stress near $19\ \text{MPa}$.

Worked examples

Three problems from this week's set, with the setups paraphrased and the method and answers shown. Full worked solutions are in the uploaded tutorial PDFs.

Sizing a two-material shaft (3.17). A steel section $AB$ (allowable $90\ \text{MPa}$) drives an aluminium section $BC$ (allowable $60\ \text{MPa}$, diameter $50\ \text{mm}$). The aluminium, with the lower allowable stress, governs the applied torque. Setting $\tau = TR/J$ to $60\ \text{MPa}$ at $R = 25\ \text{mm}$:

$$60\times10^{6} = \frac{T(0.025)}{\tfrac12\pi(0.025)^4} \;\Rightarrow\; T = 1.47\ \text{kN·m}$$

Feeding that torque into the steel section at its own $90\ \text{MPa}$ limit fixes $R_{AB} = 21.8\ \text{mm}$, so the minimum diameter is $d_{AB} \approx 43.7\ \text{mm}$.

A composite shaft (3.52). A steel core ($R = 27\ \text{mm}$, $G = 77\ \text{GPa}$) is bonded inside an aluminium jacket ($R = 36\ \text{mm}$, $G = 27\ \text{GPa}$) and carries $T = 4\ \text{kN·m}$ over $2.5\ \text{m}$. This is indeterminate: equilibrium gives $T_{\text{Al}} + T_{\text{st}} = 4$, and compatibility $\phi_{\text{Al}} = \phi_{\text{st}}$ gives $T_{\text{Al}} = 0.76\,T_{\text{st}}$. Solving, $T_{\text{st}} = 2.28\ \text{kN·m}$ and $T_{\text{Al}} = 1.72\ \text{kN·m}$, from which $\tau = Tr/J$ gives a peak of $73.7\ \text{MPa}$ in the steel and $34.3\ \text{MPa}$ in the aluminium, with an end rotation of about $5^\circ$.

Yielding and residual twist (3.159/3.160). An elastoplastic shaft ($\tau_y = 86\ \text{MPa}$, $G = 27\ \text{GPa}$, $d = 24\ \text{mm}$, $L = 1.8\ \text{m}$) carries $T = 285\ \text{N·m}$. First yield occurs at $T_y = 233\ \text{N·m}$, so the shaft is partly plastic; the elastoplastic torque equation gives an elastic-core radius $r_y = 8.35\ \text{mm}$ and a twist of $39.3^\circ$. Removing the torque, the elastic springback of $33.4^\circ$ leaves a permanent twist of $5.9^\circ$ and a residual surface stress of about $19\ \text{MPa}$ opposing the original load.
